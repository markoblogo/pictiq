1. Line-by-line reading

My best overall reading is a short transaction at a kiosk/shop counter, with the two speakers alternating lines (Line 1 = A, Line 2 = B, Line 3 = A, Line 4 = B).

Line 1 — bottle + question mark
 "A bottle/drink of water?" — either a request ("Do you have water?") or an offer ("Would you like a bottle?"). The question mark is clearly the interrogative marker, so this line is a question about the bottle.

Line 2 — check + bottle + snowflake + coins
 "Yes — a cold bottle — [and it costs / what does it cost]." The checkmark affirms, the bottle carries over the topic, the snowflake modifies it as cold/chilled, and the coin icon raises the money aspect. Without a question mark it reads as a statement of fact rather than a question: "Yes, cold bottle, it costs money."

Line 3 — "II" + "I" + bottle + cards + question mark
 Most likely: "Two-something, one bottle — by card?" The two stroke symbols look like counters or digits (2 and 1), so this is plausibly a price (2 and 1 → e.g. 2.10, or "two-one") attached to one bottle, followed by a question about card payment. Alternative reading: "Two? One? — bottle — card?" i.e. clarifying quantity and then payment method.

Line 4 — check + striped square + "I" + coins
 "Yes — scan/barcode — one — cash." The affirmation accepts, the striped box most plausibly means a barcode/scanning, "I" is one item, and the coins close the exchange with cash rather than card. So: "Yes, scan it, one, paying cash."

Whole dialogue, freely rendered:
 A: "Water?"
 B: "Yes — cold one — that'll cost."
 A: "It's 2.10 for one bottle. Card?"
 B: "Yes — scan it — just one — I'll pay cash."

2. Symbol-by-symbol

Bottle — the topic item: water/drink/bottle. Repeated in three lines, which makes it the anchor noun.

Question mark — turns the whole line into a question. It appears at the end both times, which suggests a sentence-final marker rather than an interrogative word.

Checkmark — affirmation: yes / agreed / correct. Line-initial both times, which fits a response marker.

Snowflake — cold, chilled, refrigerated. As a modifier next to the bottle.

Coin stack with $ — money, cash, price, payment.

Card stack — card payment (a contrasting payment method to the coins).

"II" and "I" — numerals or counters: two and one.

Striped square (five bars) — either a barcode/scanning action, or the numeral five as tallies.

3. Confident about

The overall genre: a brief service/purchase exchange about buying a chilled bottled drink.

The question mark as a sentence-final question marker, and the checkmark as a sentence-initial affirmation. Their positions are consistent.

The bottle as the persistent topic, the snowflake as "cold" modifying it.

The coins vs. cards contrast being about two payment methods, and the last line settling on cash.

Turn structure alternating between two speakers: question → answer → question → answer.

How it was built

"I need a taxi" → move_taxi alone. Rule 2 (zero-intent) says pointing at an object tile already means "I need this / where is this", so no separate "need" or "I" operator exists or is required. The handbook lists move_taxi as "private transport now", which is exactly the sense wanted.

"to the train station" → move_public as a qualifier under Rule 6 (compound: BASE + QUALIFIER). Core has no station tile, but move_public is explicitly described as generic cover for bus/metro/train, so move_taxi + move_public reads as "taxi → to public transport / to the station". I deliberately did not use place_airport: it belongs to the Paris pack, not Core, and it names the wrong destination.

"two people" → qty_2, placed after the object per Rule 5 (quantity follows the object: WHAT → HOW MUCH). Core has no person tile, so the count attaches to the taxi and the head-count reading comes from context — a passenger standing at a taxi with two fingers' worth of quantity is not usually asking for two taxis.

"How much will it cost?" → money_coins + punct_question. The handbook gives this pattern directly: money_coins is the most general money concept and with the question tile reads as "money / where to pay / how much?" Kept on its own line because Rule 7 prefers 1–3 tiles and says multiple sentences should be multiple lines.

The question tile on line 1 converts the zero-intent request into "can I / is there", per Rule 3.

What I had to drop

"three bags" — omitted entirely. Core has no luggage or bag tile (the luggage tag in Chapter 5 is an object, not a lexicon entry), and no generic "object/thing" tile exists to hang qty_2 + qty_1 on. A bare quantity tile with no preceding object breaks the placement rule and would most likely be misread as more passengers. The handbook's own guidance applies here: don't invent a tile, let context carry it — in a real taxi situation the bags are visible on the pavement.

"three" — unrepresentable as an exact number anyway. Core quantity is only 1, 2, 5, plus, minus. qty_2 + qty_1 is not a documented composition (only qty_5 is stated to repeat, for "many").

"I", "need", future tense, "will it" — no equivalents exist and none are needed; zero-intent supplies the intent and Pictiq has no tense.

Known ambiguity

qty_2 after a compound could be read as "two taxis" rather than "two passengers" — the handbook only defines quantity following a single object, not a base+qualifier pair. If that risk matters, drop qty_2 from the tiles and show two fingers instead; Chapter 3 explicitly recommends fingers for exact counts in face-to-face use.
