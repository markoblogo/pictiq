## Working frame

Children’s books are useful for Pictiq primarily as staged narrative experiments. They may become products later, but the current archive preserves them as a way to test comprehension, composition, omission, and vocabulary pressure.

## Staged complexity hypothesis

| Stage | Format | Pictiq pressure | Example research question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Single icon plus real-world illustration | one-symbol recognition in context | Can a viewer infer a single-symbol meaning from a scene? |
| 1 | Simple 2–3 tile compositions | repeated local grammar patterns | Does composition become understandable through repeated use? |
| 2 | Pictiq plus natural-language text | dual coding and labels | Does adding text help or hinder symbol learning? |
| 3 | Full narrative/comic use | dialogue, thought, sequence, emotion | Can Pictiq support narrative interaction without excessive lexical expansion? |

## Research questions

- Can a viewer infer single-symbol meaning from context?
- Which relations are hardest: quantity, negation, direction, evaluation, possession-like association, sequence?
- Does repeated story use teach composition better than isolated explanation?
- Which vocabulary gaps appear in child-oriented scenes?
- Can Pictiq support dialogue without adding WHO/I/YOU/ASK/ANSWER too early?
- What visual distinctions are needed for thought vs speech?
- How much can natural-language captions do before they stop testing Pictiq itself?

## Vocabulary pressure protocol

Children’s narrative may create pressure for concepts such as sleep, play, fear, joy, sadness, hug, comfort, and everyday routine concepts.

Do not create a “kids pack” automatically. Use the same method as Odyssey:

1. Real scene.
2. Current Pictiq attempt.
3. Composition.
4. Semantic compression.
5. Intentional omission.
6. Actual gap.
7. Candidate primitive only if the gap is recurring and material.
