Children can learn to associate visual symbols with objects, actions, routines, and some multi-symbol messages before conventional literacy—but this does **not** show that they acquire a general visual language automatically. The evidence supports staged learning when symbols are taught in meaningful routines, modeled by adults, and paired with speech or text. It does not support assuming that children who understand spoken sentences will naturally understand or produce equivalent two- or three-symbol sequences.

For Pictiq, the most defensible experiment is a scaffolded progression: **single concrete symbol → highly constrained two-symbol relation → three-symbol event frame → symbol-plus-text bridge → repeated narrative/dialogue use**. Treat each phase as a distinct hypothesis, not as proof of the next.

## **Do not collapse the evidence**

Several research traditions are relevant, but they test different abilities.

| Evidence area | What it actually tests | What it can support for Pictiq | What it does not establish |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Visual schedules | Following familiar activity sequences | Pictures can cue routines, transitions, and independent completion of steps | General symbolic grammar, abstract communication, novel sentence production |
| AAC symbols | Expressive/receptive communication using aided symbols | Children can learn symbol–referent links; instruction can support multi-symbol expression | That all children, or non-AAC users, will intuitively parse pictographic syntax |
| Rebus reading | Reading text mixed with pictures | Pictures can support word meaning, motivation, vocabulary, and early participation in print | That picture substitution teaches conventional decoding or general written language |
| Logographic literacy | Learning a real writing system such as Chinese characters | Visual-symbol learning can coexist with phonology, morphology, and orthographic knowledge | That logographs are “just pictures,” or that a pictorial system avoids literacy learning demands |
| Comics and picture narratives | Understanding sequences of panels and event continuity | Children can learn sequential visual narrative conventions and infer events | That a sequence of isolated icons has the same grammar as a comic |
| Pictogram literacy | Recognizing and interpreting visual signs | Concrete symbols can support communication and task completion | Cross-cultural universality, abstract syntax, or safety-critical understanding without testing |
| Spoken-language acquisition | Learning naturally occurring language through social interaction | Children can learn compositional systems through repeated meaningful use | That a designed symbolic code will be acquired without explicit conventions, modeling, and feedback |

The important distinction is between:

Recognizing an image:  
“That is a cup.”

Using a symbol:  
“Cup means I want a drink.”

Reading a symbol sequence:  
“Person \+ drink \+ water means someone drinks water.”

Producing a sequence:  
Selecting symbols in order to communicate a new proposition.

Using narrative grammar:  
Understanding continuity, causality, viewpoint, time, intention,  
and reference across multiple panels or dialogue turns.

These are related abilities, but not interchangeable.

## **What the evidence supports**

### **Visual schedules: strong for routines, not evidence of language**

Visual schedules use pictures, photographs, line drawings, objects, or written words in sequence to show upcoming activities or steps in a routine. They are widely used with autistic children and can support understanding of activities, following directions, transitions, independence, and planning.\[[raisingchildren.net](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/therapies-guide/visual-supports)\]

A recent review found good evidence that visual schedules, often combined with prompting and reinforcement, can improve on-task behavior for autistic learners; six reviewed studies met strong criteria and one met acceptable criteria under the cited evaluation standard. Other summaries characterize visual supports/activity schedules as evidence-based strategies that can help people plan for upcoming events and learn task sequences.\[[tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20473869.2024.2402124)\]\[[asatonline](https://asatonline.org/for-parents/learn-more-about-specific-treatments/activity-schedules/)\]

This strongly supports a Pictiq stage such as:

\[WAKE\]  
→ \[TOILET\]  
→ \[WASH HANDS\]  
→ \[BREAKFAST\]  
→ \[SCHOOL\]

or:

\[CHECK\]  
→ \[PACK\]  
→ \[GO OUT\]

However, a visual schedule is usually a **procedural cue system**, not a generative language. Its meaning is anchored in a known routine, setting, and adult support. The child need not infer a grammar such as actor, tense, negation scope, possession, modality, or causal relation.

### **AAC: evidence for learning symbols, with instruction**

AAC research gives the most direct evidence for learning visual symbols as communicative tools. A systematic review of direct AAC interventions identified 15 studies addressing instructional strategies for graphic-symbol learning; aided modeling was the most studied strategy and was considered evidence-based. The review also found strong evidence for narrative-based interventions and mand-model procedures in supporting graphic-symbol learning and aided language development.\[[journals.sagepub](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659018755524)\]\[[eric.ed](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1170039)\]

A broader mega-review of AAC research similarly concluded that aided AAC modeling can be considered an evidence-based practice, and that narrative-based and mand-model interventions have strong support for facilitating graphic-symbol learning and aided language acquisition.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8009928/)\]

Aided modeling means that the communication partner points to or selects the symbols while speaking. Instead of expecting a child to infer that a picture board is language, the adult demonstrates its use in real interactions:

Adult says:  
“You want more juice?”

Adult models:  
\[WANT\] \[MORE\] \[JUICE\]

Child may later select:  
\[MORE\] \[JUICE\]

This is highly relevant to Pictiq. It suggests that a child-oriented Pictiq experiment should not test symbol comprehension as an isolated puzzle. It should include:

* Repeated adult modeling.  
* Spoken-language pairing.  
* Immediate meaningful consequences.  
* Opportunities for the child to initiate, not only respond.  
* Simple repair when a message is incomplete or out of order.  
* Repeated use in play, routines, story, and social interaction.

Research also shows that children with developmental disabilities and significant receptive/expressive delays can learn new graphic-symbol–referent relationships through computer-based experience. Children tended to learn symbols more readily when they already understood the vocabulary item, although prior vocabulary knowledge did not fully determine learning success.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7673660/)\]

That supports a Pictiq principle:

> Introduce a symbol first for a concept the child already knows in speech or daily experience. Do not use a new Pictiq symbol as the only route to teach an unfamiliar abstract concept.

### **Two- and three-symbol combinations: possible, but difficult**

The most cautionary evidence concerns composition. A study of three- and four-year-old children without disabilities asked them to convert simple spoken sentences into graphic-symbol sequences and to interpret three-symbol sequences by choosing among four photographs. Few performed as expected: only one child succeeded in the transposition task, and about one-third succeeded in interpretation. The authors concluded that, despite mastering corresponding spoken-language structures, children at this age had not yet developed the skills required to deal with graphic-symbol utterances in the tested format.\[[cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/preschoolaged-children-have-difficulty-constructing-and-interpreting-simple-utterances-composed-of-graphic-symbols/DDFB53C9AEA0FFF6851D8438908A0911)\]

This is an important correction to the intuitive claim that “children understand pictures, therefore they understand pictographic sentences.”

A related AAC study of three- and four-year-old children constructing 2–3 word symbol utterances found that common difficulties were not necessarily choosing the wrong vocabulary. Children often omitted needed symbols or inverted symbol order; error patterns differed by semantic-syntactic frame. When children revised their messages, the revisions generally improved accuracy.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6570555/)\]

Another study of typically developing Hebrew-speaking four- and five-year-olds found that semantic-syntactic representation through AAC graphic symbols differed from their spoken-language production.\[[frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.702652/full)\]

These findings support a staged Pictiq experiment, but only with careful expectations:

Supported:  
Children can learn some multi-symbol combinations.

Not supported:  
Children will automatically map spoken sentence grammar onto icon order.

Likely learning problems:  
\- Omitting an actor, action, or object.  
\- Reversing actor/action/object order.  
\- Treating symbols as a list of salient objects.  
\- Using a familiar symbol association instead of its grammatical role.  
\- Failing to understand that order changes meaning.  
\- Assuming adjacent tiles are merely related rather than syntactically linked.

For Pictiq, do not begin with open-ended three-tile “sentences.” Begin with narrow semantic contrasts where order and role are observable.

Stage target:  
\[PERSON\] \[EAT\] \[APPLE\]

Contrast:  
\[PERSON\] \[EAT\] \[APPLE\]  
versus  
\[APPLE\] \[EAT\] \[PERSON\]

Task:  
Choose the matching picture or act it out.

Better early alternative:  
\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
versus  
\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[WATER\]

This varies only the desired object while preserving a learned request frame.

The first experiment tests word order and roles. The second tests slot substitution in a stable pragmatic frame. They should not be treated as the same cognitive demand.

### **Rebus materials: useful bridge, not proof of independent visual reading**

Rebus books mix text and pictures, replacing selected words—often concrete nouns—with images. They can let early readers participate in stories before they can decode all printed words and can reinforce links among spoken vocabulary, picture meaning, and print.\[[eisenhowerlibrary](https://eisenhowerlibrary.org/use-rebus-books-to-help-promote-literacy/)\]

Older experimental work reported that children found learning to “read” rebus material easier than traditional orthography. But rebus reading is generally not a full visual language. The surrounding sentence is still conventional text, and the images usually replace a small, predictable subset of words.\[[files.eric.ed](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED046631.pdf)\]

“I see a \[DOG\].”

The child can use:  
\- sentence rhythm,  
\- adult read-aloud,  
\- picture recognition,  
\- repeated phrasing,  
\- expectation that the image stands for a noun.

This does not require:  
\- productive icon grammar,  
\- systematic word order,  
\- explicit role marking,  
\- abstract relation symbols,  
\- independent sentence composition.

For Pictiq, rebuses provide good evidence for a **symbol \+ text bridge**:

\[APPLE\] apple  
\[WATER\] water  
\[WASH HANDS\] wash hands  
\[GO\] go  
\[HELP\] help

Then:

\[I\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
I want an apple.

The picture helps ground meaning; the text gradually reveals how a spoken phrase maps to print. But a Pictiq experiment should not claim that this teaches alphabetic reading by itself. Conventional reading requires learning orthographic forms and sound–symbol relationships; pictures can support vocabulary and comprehension but should not replace attention to written words.

### **Logographic recognition: visual form is not “picture reading”**

Chinese character learning is sometimes invoked as evidence that children can read visual symbols before alphabetic text. The analogy is limited.

Chinese characters are a writing system with conventional forms, phonological links, morphological structure, and learned orthographic regularities. Research on Chinese character recognition finds that visual skills alone are not uniquely associated with character reading across grades; phonological processing, morphological awareness, and rapid naming matter, while orthographic knowledge becomes increasingly important with reading experience.\[[onlinelibrary.wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9817.2010.01460.x)\]

This means:

A Chinese character is not a small illustration.  
Reading Chinese is not simply recognizing pictures.  
Logographic literacy still requires repeated learning of conventions,  
form, sound, meaning, and written-language structure.

Pictiq can borrow the idea that stable visual forms can carry conventional meaning, but it should not cite logographic literacy as proof that its pictograms will be universally intuitive or naturally acquired without instruction.

The useful Pictiq lesson is instead:

* Form consistency matters.  
* Repeated visual conventions become learned.  
* Morphological/compositional structure can help.  
* A visual system becomes literate practice through exposure, teaching, feedback, and social use.

### **Comics and visual narratives: sequential literacy develops**

Comics, picture stories, and storyboards use sequences of images to convey time, action, causality, viewpoint, emotion, and narrative structure. Research argues that visual narratives are not universally transparent; their comprehension depends on fluency developed through exposure and practice with a graphic system.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093370/)\]

The developmental review reports that children around age four begin to understand cross-panel continuity and activity cues, with fuller understanding between ages five and six. This aligns with a developmental shift from describing isolated pictures toward narrating sequential events.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093370/)\]

Other work describes visual narrative comprehension as involving a “narrative grammar” that organizes conceptual information into categorical roles and hierarchical constituents, alongside the physical layout of panels. A visual-literacy intervention has also shown improvements in comprehension and inferential understanding of educational comics.\[[frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00680/full)\]\[[shura.shu.ac](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/26486/3/Verrier_ComicLiteracyIntervention\(AM\).pdf)\]

This supports Pictiq’s later narrative/dialogue phase:

Panel 1:  
\[PERSON A\] \[HAS\] \[APPLE\]

Panel 2:  
\[PERSON B\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]

Panel 3:  
\[PERSON A\] \[GIVE\] \[APPLE\] \[TO\] \[PERSON B\]

Panel 4:  
\[PERSON B\] \[HAPPY\]

But comics use more than discrete icons:

* Facial expression.  
* Body pose and gesture.  
* Background and setting.  
* Panel size and placement.  
* Motion lines and speech balloons.  
* Temporal transitions between panels.  
* Learned genre conventions.  
* Text and dialogue.

A Pictiq narrative experiment should therefore distinguish:

Sequential picture comprehension:  
Can the child arrange pictures in event order?

Pictiq compositional grammar:  
Can the child interpret the role and order of fixed tiles?

Narrative inference:  
Can the child infer a character’s goal, cause, or emotion?

Dialogue:  
Can the child track speaker, listener, question, response,  
agreement, refusal, and repair across turns?

A child can succeed at arranging comic panels but still fail to understand that `NOT` modifies an action, that `TO` identifies a recipient, or that reversing a tile sequence changes agency.

## **Implications for Pictiq stages**

### **Stage 1: Single symbol**

**Claim being tested:** Can the child learn that a stable Pictiq tile refers to a concept, object, action, state, or communicative function?

This stage is well supported when symbols are concrete, meaningful, repeatedly modeled, and linked to action or familiar vocabulary. AAC intervention evidence supports learning visual-symbol relationships, especially with aided modeling.\[[journals.sagepub](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659018755524)\]\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8009928/)\]

Start with:

Concrete entities:  
WATER  
APPLE  
BALL  
SHOE  
BOOK  
DOG  
MUM / DAD only with family-specific consent and context

High-frequency actions:  
GO  
STOP  
EAT  
DRINK  
SLEEP  
WASH  
GIVE

Immediate needs/social functions:  
MORE  
HELP  
FINISHED  
AGAIN  
YES  
NO  
WAIT  
TOILET

Do not start with:

MAYBE  
BECAUSE  
PRIVACY  
FAIR  
IMPORTANT  
ALGORITHM  
MEMORY  
PROMISE  
IF  
UNLESS  
BEFORE  
AFTER

These can enter later once a child already treats the tile system as a meaningful expressive medium.

#### **Suggested measures**

* Can the child point to the correct tile when hearing a word?  
* Can the child select the tile to request, comment, or respond?  
* Can the child recognize the symbol across size, color, context, and medium?  
* Does the child use the tile spontaneously after adult modeling?  
* Does the tile result in the intended action often enough to reinforce learning?  
* Is performance better for known vocabulary than new vocabulary?

Do not evaluate only by “Can the child name the picture?” A child may name an apple without using `[APPLE]` communicatively.

### **Stage 2: Two-symbol composition**

**Claim being tested:** Can the child understand a stable relation or slot pattern across two symbols?

This is the highest-value early Pictiq stage because it demonstrates composition without overloading working memory.

Use patterns with clear pragmatic or perceptual contrasts:

\[MORE\] \[WATER\]  
\[MORE\] \[APPLE\]  
\[WANT\] \[BALL\]  
\[WANT\] \[BOOK\]  
\[GO\] \[OUTSIDE\]  
\[GO\] \[HOME\]  
\[NO\] \[WATER\]  
\[NO\] \[DOG\]  
\[BIG\] \[BALL\]  
\[SMALL\] \[BALL\]

Prefer frames in which one tile is stable and one slot varies:

\[WANT\] \+ OBJECT  
\[MORE\] \+ OBJECT  
\[ACTION\] \+ OBJECT  
\[ATTRIBUTE\] \+ OBJECT  
\[GO\] \+ PLACE

This tests whether a child learns that tiles combine systematically rather than only as memorized whole images.

#### **Avoid premature grammar claims**

\[PERSON\] \[EAT\]

may be easier than:

\[PERSON\] \[EAT\] \[APPLE\]

but it still requires the child to understand actor/action structure.

\[NOT\] \[EAT\]

may be much harder because negation is abstract and scope-sensitive.

\[APPLE\] \[RED\]

can be easier than:

\[PERSON\] \[GIVE\] \[APPLE\]

because attribute–object relation is visually stable, while giving requires agent, object, recipient, and direction.

### **Stage 3: Three-symbol composition**

**Claim being tested:** Can the child interpret and produce a minimal event or relation frame?

The evidence says this must be treated as a learned skill. Preschoolers have shown difficulty with simple three-symbol utterances, even when their spoken language supports the equivalent sentence; errors often involve omission and order inversion.\[[cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/preschoolaged-children-have-difficulty-constructing-and-interpreting-simple-utterances-composed-of-graphic-symbols/DDFB53C9AEA0FFF6851D8438908A0911)\]\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6570555/)\]

Begin with one canonical template at a time:

Agent–action–object:  
\[PERSON\] \[EAT\] \[APPLE\]

Entity–attribute:  
\[APPLE\] \[IS\] \[RED\]

Entity–location:  
\[BALL\] \[IN\] \[BOX\]

Possessor–entity:  
\[ME\] \[HAVE\] \[BALL\]

The AAC study explicitly examined these semantic-syntactic structures—agent-action-object, entity-attribute, entity-locative, and possessor-entity—and found that errors differed by structure. That is useful for Pictiq corpus design: do not report one overall “grammar score.” Report performance separately by frame type.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6570555/)\]

#### **Recommended order**

| Order | Pictiq frame | Why it is comparatively learnable | Likely error |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| 1 | ATTRIBUTE \+ OBJECT or OBJECT \+ ATTRIBUTE | Perceptually grounded and often visible | Attribute omitted |
| 2 | ACTION \+ OBJECT | Can be enacted directly | Object/action reversal or object omission |
| 3 | PERSON \+ WANT \+ OBJECT | Strong communicative payoff | PERSON may be omitted because speaker is obvious |
| 4 | OBJECT \+ IN \+ PLACE | Can use real objects and containers | Relation tile may be omitted |
| 5 | PERSON \+ ACTION \+ OBJECT | Core event frame, but requires role order | Agent/action inversion |
| 6 | PERSON \+ GIVE \+ OBJECT \+ TO \+ PERSON | Requires role, direction, recipient | Role confusion and incomplete frame |
| 7 | NOT / IF / BEFORE / AFTER frames | Abstract, scope-dependent grammar | Incorrect scope or order |

A child may correctly communicate `[WANT] [APPLE]` while consistently omitting `[ME]`. That may be `CONTEXT-SUFFICIENT` in a request context, not necessarily an error. The experiment should distinguish intentional/pragmatic omission from inability to represent agency.

### **Stage 4: Symbol plus text**

**Claim being tested:** Can Pictiq support vocabulary, print awareness, and meaning-making alongside—not instead of—conventional reading?

This stage has good practical support. Rebus books allow children to engage with text-plus-image sequences before they can decode every printed word. Symbol-supported text is also used to help children visualize word meaning, connect print to vocabulary, and support independent reading experience.\[[eisenhowerlibrary](https://eisenhowerlibrary.org/use-rebus-books-to-help-promote-literacy/)\]\[[widgit](https://www.widgit.com/about-symbols/emergent-reading.htm)\]

A sensible Pictiq bridge has three display modes:

Mode A: Symbol only  
\[APPLE\]

Mode B: Symbol \+ word  
\[APPLE\] apple

Mode C: Pictiq phrase \+ sentence  
\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
I want an apple.

Then gradually vary the relationship:

\[APPLE\] apple  
\[APPLE\] pomme  
\[APPLE\] яблуко

For a multilingual setting, this can reinforce the distinction between:

* One stable semantic concept/tile.  
* Multiple spoken/written labels.  
* One or more possible sentence realizations.

#### **Safeguards**

* Keep printed words visible enough that children attend to them; do not allow pictures to do all decoding work.  
* Use shared reading: point to tiles, say the words, point to print, and invite prediction.  
* Fade symbols selectively over time if the educational goal includes conventional reading.  
* Do not replace phonics, orthographic instruction, and rich oral language with icon matching.  
* Test whether children can understand the printed word without the symbol after repeated exposure.

Pictures can aid vocabulary and comprehension, but they can also mislead if they are not a close fit for the text or if children attend only to the image. Research summaries note that pictures may initially compete with words in comprehension and need guided use so children learn to integrate both sources.\[[shanahanonliteracy](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/what-role-should-pictures-play-in-teaching-reading)\]

### **Stage 5: Narrative and dialogue**

**Claim being tested:** Can children use Pictiq symbols to track recurring entities, events, goals, causal sequence, and conversational turns?

Visual narrative research supports this as a later, teachable stage. Children increasingly understand continuity and activity cues around ages four to six, while fuller visual-narrative fluency depends on exposure and practice. Four- and five-year-olds can also retain story details, moral lessons, and new vocabulary from picture books, showing that visual narrative can support multiple forms of learning when the material is developmentally appropriate.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093370/)\]\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8570620/)\]

Start with short, repeated, causally transparent stories:

1\. \[MIRA\] \[HAS\] \[BALL\]  
2\. \[NIKO\] \[WANT\] \[BALL\]  
3\. \[MIRA\] \[GIVE\] \[BALL\] \[TO\] \[NIKO\]  
4\. \[NIKO\] \[HAPPY\]

Then add dialogue acts:

1\. \[NIKO\] \[ASK\] \[BALL?\]  
2\. \[MIRA\] \[YES\]  
3\. \[MIRA\] \[GIVE\] \[BALL\]  
4\. \[NIKO\] \[THANK YOU\]

Then add one causal or emotional inference:

1\. \[RAIN\]  
2\. \[MIRA\] \[CANNOT\] \[GO OUT\]  
3\. \[MIRA\] \[SAD\]  
4\. \[MIRA\] \[PLAY\] \[INSIDE\]

The advanced test is not merely whether the child can label each tile. It is whether they can answer:

* Who wanted the ball?  
* Who gave it?  
* Why did Mira stay inside?  
* What happened first?  
* What will happen next?  
* Did Niko ask or receive?  
* What would change if `[NO]` replaced `[YES]`?  
* Can the child create a new but grammatically valid variation?

## **Evidence-informed experiment design**

### **Separate receptive from productive learning**

A child may understand a tile sequence before being able to build it, or may memorize a familiar phrase without understanding each component.

Use at least four task types:

| Task | What it measures | Example |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Symbol recognition | Symbol–referent link | “Show me WATER.” |
| Receptive composition | Interpretation of a sequence | “Which picture shows `[DOG] [EAT] [APPLE]`?” |
| Guided production | Building a message with support | “Tell me you want the ball.” |
| Spontaneous production | Generative use in real interaction | Child selects `[MORE] [WATER]` without being prompted |
| Error repair | Understanding of form and meaning | Adult asks, “Do you mean you want the apple or the ball?” |
| Transfer | Rule learning beyond memorized examples | Child uses `[WANT] [BOOK]` after learning `[WANT] [BALL]` |
| Narrative inference | Sequence/causality understanding | “Why did the character go home?” |
| Text bridge | Relationship between tiles and print | “Can you find the word *apple* without the picture?” |

### **Use real referents before abstract images**

The most transparent early teaching sequence is:

Real object/action  
→ photograph or contextual picture  
→ stable Pictiq tile  
→ tile plus spoken word  
→ tile plus printed word  
→ tile in a two-symbol frame  
→ tile in a three-symbol frame  
→ tile in a story/dialogue

Example:

Real apple on table  
→ photo of apple  
→ \[APPLE\]  
→ adult says “apple”  
→ \[APPLE\] apple  
→ \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
→ \[ME\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
→ character in a story wants apple

This helps children learn that a symbol is not the object itself but a durable representation that can stand for it across contexts.

### **Make composition visible**

Do not assume adjacency automatically means grammar. Use stable layout conventions:

Actor slot:  
left / first tile

Action slot:  
middle / second tile

Object slot:  
right / third tile

Question:  
fixed opening or closing marker

Negation:  
separate tile with a clearly defined scope position

Time:  
fixed final position or tagged top-right modifier

Place:  
fixed final position or lower attachment

The early AAC evidence on inversion and omission makes this important. If `PERSON + EAT + APPLE` is a meaningful Pictiq frame, do not sometimes render the same relationship as `APPLE + PERSON + EAT` merely for visual balance.

### **Model, do not quiz first**

The strongest AAC evidence favors aided modeling, narrative approaches, and structured prompts. A staged Pictiq study should include substantial modeling before formal assessment:\[[eric.ed](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1170039)\]\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8009928/)\]

Adult:  
speaks naturally,  
points to the Pictiq tiles,  
uses them in the actual activity,  
responds meaningfully,  
and reformulates incomplete messages without punishment.

Example repair:

Child selects:  
\[APPLE\]

Adult models:  
“You want an apple.”  
\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[APPLE\]

Then offers:  
apple or water.

Do not treat an incomplete child message as a failure if the child successfully communicates. Record it as a pragmatic compression, then model the expandable form.

## **What not to claim**

A responsible staged Pictiq experiment should avoid these claims.

### **“Pictures are universal”**

They are not. Visual narrative comprehension depends on exposure and learned conventions, and icon interpretation varies with culture, experience, context, and graphic style.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093370/)\]

### **“Children understand symbols before they can read”**

Children can often recognize and use familiar pictures before alphabetic reading, but recognition, rebus participation, schedule-following, AAC use, and literacy are different abilities. A child may know what a dog picture means but not understand a graphic-symbol sentence or conventional written word.

### **“Graphic symbols naturally express spoken grammar”**

The evidence argues against this. Preschool children may struggle to produce or interpret three-symbol sequences even when they understand equivalent spoken structures.\[[cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/preschoolaged-children-have-difficulty-constructing-and-interpreting-simple-utterances-composed-of-graphic-symbols/DDFB53C9AEA0FFF6851D8438908A0911)\]

### **“Logographic scripts prove Pictiq is easy”**

They do not. Logographic literacy involves conventional orthography, morphology, phonology, memory, and sustained reading experience—not direct picture recognition.\[[onlinelibrary.wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9817.2010.01460.x)\]

### **“Visual schedules prove visual language acquisition”**

They show that visual cues can support routines and independent task completion. They do not demonstrate free, productive, general-purpose symbolic language.

### **“Pictiq can replace speech, text, or AAC expertise”**

No. If used with children who have communication needs, Pictiq should be developed and evaluated with speech-language therapists, AAC specialists, educators, children, and families. It should complement existing communication methods and preserve access to robust, individualized AAC where needed.

## **Proposed staged Pictiq experiment**

### **Phase 0: Symbol foundation**

**Target:** 8–12 high-frequency concrete and functional symbols.

WATER  
FOOD  
BALL  
BOOK  
GO  
STOP  
MORE  
HELP  
FINISHED  
TOILET  
YES  
NO

**Success criteria**

* Child chooses the intended tile among 3–6 alternatives.  
* Child uses selected symbols meaningfully in a routine or play activity.  
* Child recognizes the tile across color/theme/size variation.  
* Adult reports spontaneous or prompted functional use.  
* Symbol labels do not rely on the child’s ability to read text.

**Do not test yet**

negation scope  
conditionals  
time  
possession  
abstract emotions  
multi-party events

### **Phase 1: Stable two-symbol frames**

**Target:** 4–6 repeatable templates.

\[WANT\] \+ OBJECT  
\[MORE\] \+ OBJECT  
\[GO\] \+ PLACE  
\[ATTRIBUTE\] \+ OBJECT  
\[ACTION\] \+ OBJECT  
\[NO\] \+ OBJECT

**Core experiment**

Teach:

\[WANT\] \[BALL\]  
\[WANT\] \[APPLE\]  
\[WANT\] \[WATER\]

Then test transfer:

\[WANT\] \[BOOK\]

Success is not memorizing three phrases; it is applying the `WANT + OBJECT` pattern to a new familiar object.

### **Phase 2: Three-symbol semantic frames**

**Target:** small, separately tested grammar frames.

\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[OBJECT\]  
\[PERSON\] \[ACTION\] \[OBJECT\]  
\[OBJECT\] \[IN\] \[PLACE\]  
\[OBJECT\] \[IS\] \[ATTRIBUTE\]  
\[ME\] \[HAVE\] \[OBJECT\]

**Key measures**

* Correct interpretation.  
* Correct order.  
* Completeness: actor, action, object/attribute/location present where required.  
* Error type: omission, inversion, wrong symbol, pragmatic compression, or attention lapse.  
* Repair after adult recast.  
* Transfer to unseen vocabulary.

Do not aggregate all errors into one score. The AAC evidence suggests that different structures produce different error patterns.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6570555/)\]

### **Phase 3: Symbol-plus-text bridge**

**Target:** 10–20 paired concept labels and several repeatable sentences.

\[APPLE\] apple  
\[WATER\] water  
\[HELP\] help  
\[ME\] \[WANT\] \[WATER\]  
I want water.

**Test**

* Can the child match tile → spoken word?  
* Tile → print word?  
* Print word → tile?  
* Print word → real object?  
* Can the child recognize selected words when the tiles are faded/removed?  
* Does the presence of a tile improve comprehension without preventing attention to print?

For multilingual Pictiq materials, keep the semantic tile constant while changing the label:

\[WATER\] water  
\[WATER\] eau  
\[WATER\] вода

This can reinforce the difference between shared concept and language-specific word, but should not overload early learners with several labels simultaneously unless multilingual learning is the actual goal.

### **Phase 4: Narrative and dialogue**

**Target:** 3–6 panel stories with recurring Entity Symbols and a small action vocabulary.

\[MIRA\] \[HAS\] \[BALL\]  
\[NIKO\] \[WANT\] \[BALL\]  
\[MIRA\] \[GIVE\] \[BALL\] \[TO\] \[NIKO\]  
\[NIKO\] \[HAPPY\]

Then dialogue:

\[NIKO\] \[ASK\] \[BALL?\]  
\[MIRA\] \[YES\]  
\[MIRA\] \[GIVE\] \[BALL\]  
\[NIKO\] \[THANK\]

**Test**

* Event ordering.  
* Character reference.  
* Goal inference.  
* Emotion/result inference.  
* Question versus statement.  
* Response coherence.  
* Ability to create a changed ending.  
* Ability to retell with tiles, speech, text, or drawings.

Narrative competence should be assessed separately from tile grammar. A child might understand the story but produce imperfect syntax, or produce the tiles in order without inferring the character’s intention.

## **Bottom line**

The evidence genuinely supports using visual symbols with children as part of meaningful communication and learning:

* Visual schedules can support routines, transitions, and independence, especially in structured interventions.\[[raisingchildren.net](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/therapies-guide/visual-supports)\]\[[tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20473869.2024.2402124)\]  
* AAC research supports explicit instruction in graphic-symbol learning, particularly aided modeling, narrative-based approaches, and structured prompting.\[[journals.sagepub](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659018755524)\]\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8009928/)\]  
* Children can learn symbol–referent relationships, including new visual symbols, especially when tied to known vocabulary and meaningful activity.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7673660/)\]  
* Rebus and symbol-supported text can provide a bridge between pictures, spoken words, and print.\[[eisenhowerlibrary](https://eisenhowerlibrary.org/use-rebus-books-to-help-promote-literacy/)\]\[[files.eric.ed](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED046631.pdf)\]  
* Children can develop visual-narrative comprehension, but it depends on exposure and practice rather than being universally automatic.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7093370/)\]\[[frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00680/full)\]

The evidence also sets a firm boundary:

* Three- and four-year-olds may struggle with even simple graphic-symbol sequences despite understanding equivalent spoken language.\[[cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/preschoolaged-children-have-difficulty-constructing-and-interpreting-simple-utterances-composed-of-graphic-symbols/DDFB53C9AEA0FFF6851D8438908A0911)\]  
* Production errors in multi-symbol AAC messages often concern omissions and word order, which means Pictiq composition must be explicitly taught and carefully tested.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6570555/)\]  
* Visual schedules, rebus books, comics, AAC, and logographic reading are related but distinct phenomena; none independently validates a general-purpose pictographic language.

The right staged Pictiq hypothesis is therefore:

Children may learn Pictiq progressively when its symbols are stable,  
concrete, socially modeled, communicatively useful, and paired with  
speech/text—not because pictures automatically become language.

That is a rigorous and promising basis for testing:

single symbol  
→ stable two-symbol frame  
→ constrained three-symbol semantic frame  
→ symbol \+ text bridge  
→ repeated narrative and dialogue

with clear performance measures, explicit grammar conventions, and no claim that one successful stage guarantees competence in the next.
