# How Pictogram and Symbol Comprehension Is Formally Tested: ISO 9186, AAC Research, and Practical Methods for Small Projects

## Overview

Formal evaluation of graphical symbols spans four overlapping but methodologically distinct traditions: international standardization (ISO 9186, ISO 7001, ISO 3864/7010), national/industrial safety-sign regulation (ANSI Z535), augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) research (transparency/translucency studies, ARASAAC norms), and general HCI icon-usability practice (recognition/interpretation/context testing as codified by practitioners such as the Nielsen Norman Group). Each tradition asks a superficially similar question — "does this symbol mean what we intend?" — but operationalizes it differently, uses different sample-size conventions, and has attracted different methodological critiques. This report works through the ISO 9186 family in detail, situates it against ANSI Z535 and AAC-specific practice, reviews peer-reviewed critiques and alternative methods, separates the three constructs of recognition, comprehension, and context-of-use, covers cross-cultural testing methodology, and closes with realistic recommendations and five concrete experiment designs for a small open-source pictogram project (Pictiq).

Sources are explicitly divided throughout into **Category A — Primary standards documents and peer-reviewed academic studies** and **Category B — Secondary commentary, summaries, blog posts, or practitioner writeups**, with each citation labeled at first use and summarized in a table at the end.

## 1. ISO 9186 in Detail

### 1.1 What ISO 9186 is and how it is structured

ISO 9186, "Graphical symbols — Test methods," is issued in three parts, each testing a different property of a candidate symbol rather than three versions of the same test [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014 standard text](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]:

- **Part 1 — Method for testing comprehensibility.** Measures the proportion of respondents who correctly infer a symbol's intended meaning when shown the symbol with no explanatory text, and expresses this as a percentage of correct answers per symbol variant [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]. This is the "comprehension test" most people mean when they cite ISO 9186.
- **Part 2 — Method for testing perceptual quality.** Measures whether the visual *elements* that constitute a symbol are correctly identifiable — i.e., can respondents name what they see (a hand, an arrow, a flame) — independent of whether they infer the intended abstract meaning [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)].
- **Part 3 — Method for testing symbol referent association.** Measures whether people who have been trained to be familiar with a specialized referent (e.g., an aircraft-cockpit control or an unfamiliar chemical hazard) can correctly associate a symbol with that referent once they already know what the referent means. This test is designed specifically for symbols whose referents are not general public knowledge and require familiarity training before testing [Category A: [ISO 9186-3:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59882/69c510677bef4414a012fe58d395eb8b/ISO-9186-3-2014.pdf)].

Note on nomenclature: the task explicitly asked about a "Part 2 focus group method." The retrieved primary text of ISO 9186-2:2008 does **not** describe a focus-group discussion method; it specifies an individual, quantitative "naming the elements" task administered one respondent at a time, with an independent group of at least 25 respondents required per symbol size tested, and at least two symbol sizes (80 mm × 80 mm plus one or more smaller sizes) [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)]. Genuine qualitative focus-group methodology for symbol testing exists in the literature (e.g., a focus-group study of vehicle-automation iconography) but is not the ISO 9186-2 procedure itself [Category A: [Springer, focus group study on vehicle automation iconography](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42454-021-00034-2)]. This distinction matters for anyone citing "ISO 9186 Part 2" as a qualitative method — it is not one.

### 1.2 Part 1: exact procedure

The comprehension test in ISO 9186-1 works as follows [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]:

1. Each respondent is shown **one symbol variant for one referent** (never all variants of the same referent to the same person, to avoid contamination).
2. The respondent is asked: **"What do you think this symbol means?"**
3. If the response needs clarification, or the referent implies an action, the respondent is also asked: **"What action should you take in response to this symbol?"**
4. Symbols may be presented on paper or on a computer/internet screen, with print pages at least A5 size and on-screen images at least 28 mm × 28 mm at a viewing distance of 400–700 mm.
5. A maximum of three variants per referent is recommended, and no more than 15 referents should be tested in one set to avoid respondent fatigue.
6. Booklets must randomize symbol order: for every 50 booklets, at least 10 different random orders must be used, with no more than 5 of 50 booklets sharing an identical order when 10 or more referents are tested.
7. Each response is coded by independent judges into categories. The 1989 edition specifies seven response categories: (1) correct understanding certain, (2) correct understanding likely, (3) correct understanding marginally likely, (4) meaning stated is the opposite of intended, (5) response is wrong, (6) "don't know," (7) no response [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)]. A later, simplified scheme reported in secondary literature uses four categories: correct, wrong, wrong-and-opposite ("critical confusion"), don't know, no response [Category B: [Galea, Greenwich signage-comprehension survey PDF](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/34049/1/34049%20GALEA_Signage_Comprehension_Survey_(Sup-Info)_2021.pdf)].
8. **Scoring:** "Each variant is scored in terms of the percentage of correct answers" (Clause 4, ISO 9186-1) [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]. In the older, more explicit 1989 scoring formula: for each variant, add the percentage of category-1 and category-2 responses to produce the comprehension score; the variant with the highest total is the most comprehensible [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)].

### 1.3 The 66% and 85% thresholds — where they actually come from

This is the most frequently mischaracterized part of ISO 9186 in practitioner writing, so the primary-source language is quoted directly.

- The **66% criterion** originates in ISO 9186:1989, Clause 5.5.7: **"If the comprehension score for this variant exceeds 66%, then this variant may be used to define the standard image content... For critical referents (e.g. safety symbols) the 66% criterion shall be rigorously adhered to. For less important referents the criterion may be relaxed by including category 3 responses in the cumulative value in order to comply with the 66% criterion."** [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)]. Contrary to the common assumption that 66% is the "easy" public-information threshold and 85% is the "strict" safety threshold, the original 1989 text applies the 66% figure specifically and "rigorously" to safety-critical referents, with lower-stakes referents given some scoring flexibility to still reach 66%.
- Later editions of ISO 9186-1/-2 do not hard-code a universal percentage at all. Instead, they delegate the acceptance threshold to the adopting standards body: **"The organization to which the graphical symbol is submitted as a proposal for standardization... will specify whether the graphical symbol has to obtain a particular correct identification percentage before it can be accepted for standardization, and, if so, what this percentage is"** (ISO 9186-2:2008, Clause 4, Note 2), adding that criteria can vary by presentation size and that **"For safety symbols, for instance, the criterion might be stricter than for public information symbols"** (Clause 6.1.5, Note 1) [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)].
- The **85% figure** is empirically documented as the **ANSI Z535.3 safety-symbol acceptance criterion**, not an ISO 9186 threshold: ANSI Z535.3 requires **at least 85% correct responses and no more than 5% "critical confusions"** (responses that convey the opposite meaning) [Category A: [IIID Symbol Comprehension Testing Protocol PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Symbol-Comprehension-Testing-Protocol.pdf)] [Category A: [IIID, Olgyay, "Building on Test Results to Design Safety Symbols" PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Olgyay-Nora--Building-on-Test-Results-to-Design-Safety-Symbols.pdf)]. This conflation of "the ISO threshold" and "the ANSI threshold" into a single "66%/85%" pair is common in secondary sources but the two numbers belong to two different standards bodies with different scoring rules. A peer-reviewed comparison of teleconferencing-interface symbols against both criteria found that **only 7 of 21 tested symbols reached ISO's ~66% suggested comprehension level, and only 2 of 21 reached ANSI's 85% level**, concretely illustrating how much harder the ANSI bar is to clear [Category A: [Chan & Ng, "The Relevance of Error Analysis in Graphical Symbols" (CIOP)](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)].
- A frequently-cited peer-reviewed study by Foster directly tested whether 66% or 85% should be the international criterion, comparing an ATM/cash-machine symbol across the UK, Korea, and Iran using an ISO 9186:2001 "comprehension judgement" test (in that variant, respondents *estimate* what percentage of the general population would understand each variant, rather than personally guessing the meaning). The paper concludes the international criterion "should be 66% rather than 85%," while also finding that Iranian respondents gave systematically lower comprehensibility estimates than UK or Korean respondents, "emphasizing the need to collect data from different countries" [Category A: [Foster, "International assessment of judged symbol comprehensibility," Applied Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207590444000258)].
- More recent applied studies most often cite the rounded figure **66.7%** as "the ISO criterion" [Category A: [Comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms in sub-Saharan migrants, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38158019/)] or **67%** attributed to ISO 3864/ISO 9186-1 in eye-tracking pharmaceutical-pictogram research [Category A: [Evaluation of pharmaceutical packaging pictograms via eye tracking, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36304680/)], underscoring that the "66% vs 85%" framing survives in applied literature as an approximate ISO-vs-ANSI shorthand even though the original ISO clause language ties 66% to safety-critical referents specifically.

### 1.4 Sample size conventions in ISO-compliant studies

- **ISO 9186:1989, Clause 5.5.2** specifies: **"Conduct the comprehension test in at least four countries,"** with **"at least 100 respondents in each country, with an approximately equal number of respondents from each age group"** (age bands: 15–30, 31–50, over 50) [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)]. This implies a minimum of roughly 400 respondents across the full ISO panel for a symbol set.
- Both **ISO 9186-1 and ANSI Z535.3** are cited in secondary literature as requiring a minimum of **50 respondents per candidate symbol** in national/single-market testing contexts, distinct from the 400-respondent, four-country ISO panel figure [Category B: [Galea, Greenwich signage-comprehension survey PDF](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/34049/1/34049%20GALEA_Signage_Comprehension_Survey_(Sup-Info)_2021.pdf)].
- **ANSI Z535.3** independently requires a **minimum of 50 test subjects**, and its 2011 revision added a formal "table of equivalent scoring" (Annex B, Table B1, "Comprehension Equivalencies") for cases where fewer than 50 subjects can practically be tested, plus explicit safeguards for internet-administered testing [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview, ANSI webstore](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)]. An IIID field validation of new safety-symbol designs against Z535 used 58 subjects, deliberately oversampling industrial workers (target 40%, actual 89%) and people over 50 (target 33%, actual 34%) [Category A: [IIID, Olgyay, "Building on Test Results to Design Safety Symbols" PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Olgyay-Nora--Building-on-Test-Results-to-Design-Safety-Symbols.pdf)].
- **ISO 9186-2 (perceptual quality)** requires "an independent group of at least 25 respondents" for each symbol size tested, with a minimum of two sizes [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)].
- **ISO 9186-3 (referent association)** requires a familiarity-training pre-screen: only respondents who correctly demonstrate understanding of *all* referents in a familiarization list are admitted into the actual test group, which restricts and typically shrinks the effective sample relative to Part 1 [Category A: [ISO 9186-3:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59882/69c510677bef4414a012fe58d395eb8b/ISO-9186-3-2014.pdf)].

### 1.5 How symbols pass or fail

A symbol variant passes when its comprehension score (percentage of correct/near-correct responses under the applicable coding scheme) meets or exceeds the threshold set by the adopting standards body for that symbol class — historically 66% for ISO safety-critical referents, and separately 85% with a 5%-critical-confusion ceiling under ANSI Z535.3 [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)] [Category A: [IIID Symbol Comprehension Testing Protocol PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Symbol-Comprehension-Testing-Protocol.pdf)]. Where multiple variants of the same referent are tested, the highest-scoring variant that clears the threshold is recommended as the standard image; ties are broken by whichever variant has fewer outright-wrong ("category 5") responses [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)]. Real-world outcomes show failure is common: in a study of teleconferencing icons, only 7 of 21 symbols met the ISO-level bar and only 2 of 21 met ANSI's [Category A: [Chan & Ng, CIOP PDF](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)]; in a study of pharmaceutical pictograms tested on sub-Saharan migrants newly arrived in Europe, **none of 10 tested US Pharmacopeia pictograms reached the 66.7% threshold** [Category A: [Comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms in sub-Saharan migrants, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38158019/)].

### 1.6 Related standards: ISO 7001, ANSI Z535, ISO 3864

| Standard | Scope | Relationship to ISO 9186 |

### 1.7 How AAC practice differs from general signage standards

AAC symbol research and practice diverge from ISO/ANSI signage testing in three important ways.

First, AAC evaluation typically measures graded, continuous properties (transparency ratings, translucency ratings, name agreement percentages, iconicity indices) rather than a binary pass/fail against a single percentage threshold, because AAC symbols are chosen from competing commercial/open symbol sets (ARASAAC, Picture Communication Symbols/Boardmaker, Bliss, Widgit) for individualized communication boards rather than standardized for universal public deployment [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, transparency/translucency indices for 1,525 ARASAAC pictograms](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].

Second, AAC research samples are frequently small, clinical, and population-specific (e.g., 40 nonspeaking participants with intellectual disability comparing 11 symbol types; 24 children with language/autism diagnoses comparing ARASAAC/SPC/Bliss learnability) rather than the ISO panel's large, general-population, multi-country design [Category A: [Comparison of Symbol Transparency in Nonspeaking Persons, ASHA journals](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jshd.5402.131)] [Category A: [ARASAAC, Cabello & Mazón iconicity/ease-of-learning study summary](https://aulaabierta.arasaac.org/en/iconocity-and-ease-of-learning-of-the-symbols-pictographic-of-arasaac)].

Third, ARASAAC in particular has built a large, freely available *normative dataset* rather than a pass/fail certification: its 2018 normative study collected name-agreement, familiarity, visual-complexity, image-agreement, and naming-response-time data across roughly 219 university students, then a 2024 follow-up collected transparency (naming-in-isolation) and translucency (rating with label present) data for all 1,525 pictograms using online tasks with 60–65 participants per subtask, deliberately built to be reused as an open psycholinguistic resource rather than a one-time compliance check [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency norms](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)] [Category A: [Norms for a Pictographic System: ARASAAC, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305113/)]. PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System) validation research, by contrast, is not focused on individual-symbol comprehensibility scores at all — it is an intervention-efficacy literature (randomized/pilot trials measuring communicative outcomes like requesting behavior in children with autism), which is a fundamentally different evaluative question from "can a naive viewer guess what this icon means" [Category A: [Travis & Geiger, PECS South African pilot study, SAGE Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659009349971)]. Widgit and Boardmaker/PCS appear in the literature primarily as comparison conditions inside AAC transparency/iconicity studies rather than as subjects of their own independent ISO-style comprehension certification [Category A: [Comparison of Symbol Transparency in Nonspeaking Persons, ASHA journals](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jshd.5402.131)].

## 2. Criticism and Alternatives to ISO 9186

### 2.1 Open-ended guessing vs. multiple-choice recognition

ISO 9186-1's canonical method is open-ended: respondents write or say what they think a symbol means, and independent judges code the response [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]. ANSI Z535.3's 2011 revision explicitly weighed the alternative and rejected it: multiple-choice testing was **"discouraged because it is typically less accurate than open-ended testing" for measuring comprehension**, specifically because a closed answer set constrains the range of interpretations a respondent could produce, inflating apparent comprehension relative to real-world unaided use [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview, ANSI webstore](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)]. A peer-reviewed study of teleconferencing-interface symbols used a middle-ground "cued-response association" design — a described referent paired with a forced choice among seven candidate symbols — explicitly because it better simulates a real interface where a user chooses among a visible set of icons rather than free-associating to one icon shown alone; the authors found that raw hit rates alone "cannot reveal the dynamics of how each symbol can affect the other's level [of] comprehension," i.e. that multi-choice/multi-symbol context changes what "comprehension" even measures relative to the ISO single-symbol open-ended baseline [Category A: [Chan & Ng, "The Relevance of Error Analysis in Graphical Symbols," CIOP](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)].

### 2.2 Transparency, translucency, and opacity in AAC symbol research

These three terms form a graded taxonomy used specifically in AAC research (not general signage research) to describe how strongly a symbol's form relates to its referent:

- **Transparency**: a symbol's meaning is guessable **without** the referent or a label present — sometimes literally called "guessability" — typically operationalized as a naming task where a symbol is shown alone and the participant types a single word describing it; transparency is then the proportion of respondents who produce the target name [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].
- **Translucency**: the degree of relationship between symbol and referent when the referent/label **is** given — operationalized as a rating task (commonly a 7-point scale from "no relationship" to "very strong relationship") where the label is shown beneath the symbol and the participant rates the fit [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].
- **Opacity**: the point on the transparency/translucency continuum where the symbol-referent relationship is arbitrary or unguessable even with the label present (e.g., most Blissymbols or abstract logographs); none of the retrieved primary sources provided an explicit operational task for opacity, which appears in the literature mainly as the low end of the transparency/translucency continuum rather than as its own measured construct [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].

A widely cited early empirical study directly comparing symbol sets on this dimension tested 40 nonspeaking participants with intellectual disability (some also with physical impairments or autism) across 11 symbol types — including real objects, miniatures, photographs (identical and nonidentical), Picture Communication Symbols, Picsyms, Rebus, Self-Talk, Blissymbols, and written words — and found real objects were recognized most easily, Blissymbols and written words were hardest, establishing an empirical difficulty hierarchy for AAC symbol-set selection decisions [Category A: [Comparison of Symbol Transparency in Nonspeaking Persons, ASHA journals](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jshd.5402.131)]. This 1989 study did not use "translucency" or "opacity" as terms; that fuller three-way taxonomy is more explicit in later work such as the ARASAAC norming studies [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].

### 2.3 Guessability and iconicity rating research

"Guessability" studies of pharmaceutical/consumer pictograms treat comprehension as a function of prospective-user factors (age, literacy, cultural background) and sign-design features (concreteness, familiarity, complexity), and are indexed in PubMed specifically under this framing, e.g. research on "guessability of pharmaceutical pictograms" as affected jointly by design features and user characteristics [Category A: [Effects of prospective-user factors and sign design features on guessability, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23146416/)]. Iconicity research (a broader linguistics/psycholinguistics tradition that also studies sign language and spoken-word iconicity) has converged on the finding that a **single guessing/transparency task and a separate rating-scale iconicity task capture different, complementary information** — the paper "Two measures are better than one" explicitly argues that combining iconicity ratings with guessing experiments gives "a more nuanced picture of iconicity in the lexicon" than either alone [Category A: [Winter, Sidhu, et al., "Two measures are better than one," Language and Cognition (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/article/two-measures-are-better-than-one-combining-iconicity-ratings-and-guessing-experiments-for-a-more-nuanced-picture-of-iconicity-in-the-lexicon/13EBC624830C2F879FEBAA116DFEA7B8)]. A dedicated methodological survey, "The Iconicity Toolbox," catalogues the empirical approaches used across this literature, including rating scales, guessing/naming tasks, and corpus/structural analyses [Category A: [Motamedi et al., "The Iconicity Toolbox," accepted manuscript](https://ymotamedi.github.io/iconicity_paper_accepted.pdf)].

### 2.4 Cross-cultural and low-literacy/disability generalizability critiques

Multiple peer-reviewed applied studies function as de facto critiques of ISO 9186's cross-cultural and cross-population generalizability by directly testing ISO-registered or ISO-style pictograms on non-Western, low-literacy, or migrant populations and reporting systematic failure to meet the ISO threshold:

- A study testing 10 US Pharmacopeia pictograms on 51 sub-Saharan migrants at a Temporary Stay Centre for Immigrants in Melilla, Spain found that **none of the 10 pictograms reached the 66.70% ISO comprehensibility threshold**, concluding that pharmaceutical pictograms are ineffective "when they are not designed in a culturally sensitive manner" [Category A: [Comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms in sub-Saharan migrants, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38158019/)].
- A closely related study testing 10 South African pictograms on 55 newly-arrived sub-Saharan migrants similarly found **none of the 10 pictograms met the 66.70% criterion**, with correct-response rates ranging from just 20.00% to 52.50%, and found neither health literacy nor sociodemographic variables significantly predicted comprehension — implying the failure was about symbol design/cultural fit rather than participant literacy per se [Category A: [Comprehensibility of South African pictograms by sub-Saharan African prospective-users, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37567037/)].
- Foster's original three-country study itself is a partial critique from within the ISO tradition: it found Iranian respondents gave systematically different (lower) comprehensibility judgments than UK/Korean respondents for the same symbol variants, and used this finding to argue explicitly that single-country or Western-only testing is inadequate for symbols intended for international standardization [Category A: [Foster, Applied Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207590444000258)].
- The teleconferencing-symbols study frames the generalizability problem structurally: symbols are "often designed and tested in the West but targeted for worldwide use," and the authors explicitly tested Western-designed symbols on Southeast Asian participants to probe this gap, finding markedly reduced comprehension rates relative to Western-normed expectations [Category A: [Chan & Ng, CIOP PDF](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)].

### 2.5 Alternative methodologies proposed in the literature

Beyond the ISO comprehension-judgment format, the literature proposes and uses:

- **Iconicity rating tasks** — Likert/semantic-differential scales asking participants to rate how strongly a symbol's form suggests its meaning, used as a continuous complement to binary pass/fail comprehension scoring [Category A: [Motamedi et al., "The Iconicity Toolbox"](https://ymotamedi.github.io/iconicity_paper_accepted.pdf)].
- **Name agreement tasks** — participants freely name a depicted concept; the proportion giving the modal (most common) name is the name-agreement score, a psycholinguistic norming method predating and running parallel to ISO comprehension testing, historically used for line-drawing picture-naming norms and now applied to AAC pictogram norming (e.g., the ARASAAC normative dataset reports name agreement alongside familiarity and visual complexity) [Category A: [Norms for a Pictographic System: ARASAAC, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305113/)] [Category A: [Cycowicz & Friedman, picture naming by young children, name agreement norms](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED402069.pdf)].
- **Eye-tracking studies** — measure fixation count, time-to-first-fixation, total dwell time, and heat maps over defined "areas of interest" to assess not just whether a pictogram is understood but whether and how quickly it is *noticed and processed* visually, which the open-ended comprehension test cannot capture on its own. One eye-tracking study of Slovenian service pictograms (90 participants, Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker, 250 Hz) explicitly built its simple pictogram stimuli from symbols that had already cleared the ISO 9186-1 minimum comprehension threshold, then examined how added visual complexity affected fixation patterns and comprehension accuracy on top of that ISO baseline [Category A: [Effects of Pictogram and Typeface Complexity on Visual Attention, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398171/)]. A separate eye-tracking study of 42 participants viewing pharmaceutical packaging pictograms found participants fixated on pictograms up to 95% of the time, yet comprehension still ranged only from 11.9% to 71.43%, with only pictograms validated by the International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP) reaching the roughly 67% ISO-referenced minimum — demonstrating that visual attention/salience and semantic comprehension are empirically separable outcomes [Category A: [Evaluation of pharmaceutical packaging pictograms via eye tracking, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36304680/)].
- **Longitudinal learnability studies** — an ARASAAC-commissioned study trained 24 children (with language delay, specific language disorder, or autism spectrum diagnoses) on ARASAAC, SPC (Boardmaker/PCS), or Bliss symbol sets across an initial evaluation, two training sessions, and a 7–9 day follow-up, finding ARASAAC produced the best initial performance and, along with SPC, the only sets whose learning gains were retained at follow-up — a design fundamentally different from ISO 9186's single-exposure snapshot test, because it measures learning *over repeated exposure* rather than first-encounter guessability [Category A: [ARASAAC, Cabello & Mazón iconicity/ease-of-learning study](https://aulaabierta.arasaac.org/en/iconocity-and-ease-of-learning-of-the-symbols-pictographic-of-arasaac)].
- **Focus-group / qualitative methods** — used both for iconography co-design (a focus-group study on vehicle-automation iconography) [Category A: [Iconography of vehicle automation, focus group study, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42454-021-00034-2)] and, importantly, for participatory AAC research that treats AAC users themselves as co-researchers rather than only as test subjects — an HCI paper on "Qualitative Methods for Involving AAC Users as Primary Research Participants" surveys visual-methods interviews, Talking Mats-style symbol-based interviewing, and email-based interviewing as accessible alternatives to verbal focus groups for people who cannot easily participate in a standard spoken discussion [Category A: [Qualitative Methods for Involving AAC Users as Primary Research Participants, ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3313831.3376883)].

## 3. Three Distinct Evaluative Constructs

A recurring source of confusion in both ISO documentation and popular commentary is treating "symbol testing" as one thing. The literature and practice actually distinguish at least three constructs, and conflating them produces misleading comprehension claims.

### (a) Recognition — "can you identify/name the symbol in isolation?"

This asks only whether the visual form is legible and nameable as *something*, independent of whether the viewer correctly infers the abstract intended meaning. ISO 9186-2's perceptual-quality test is the clearest formal example: it only asks respondents to "name the elements" of a symbol's image content (e.g., "that's a hand," "that's an arrow"), explicitly separate from whether the respondent correctly infers what the symbol as a whole is trying to communicate [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)]. In general icon-usability practice, the Nielsen Norman Group frames this as the question **"Does this icon look like a timer to users?"**, tested via out-of-context recognition surveys (icon shown alone, no label, ask what it is) or in-context screenshot description tasks, recommended early in a design process before interpretation or task-based testing [Category B: [Nielsen Norman Group, "How to Test Digital Icons"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-test-digital-icons/)]. AAC transparency tasks (naming a symbol shown alone) are methodologically a recognition test even though the AAC literature usually calls this "transparency" rather than "recognition" [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].

### (b) Comprehension — "can you correctly infer its intended meaning?"

This is the ISO 9186-1 core construct: given the symbol alone (with no label), can the respondent produce or select the *intended abstract referent/message*, scored as percent-correct against the 66%/85%-style thresholds discussed in Section 1 [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)]. It can be operationalized as open-ended guessing (ISO/ANSI default) or multiple-choice/cued selection (used in some HCI and interface-icon studies, though ANSI explicitly discourages multiple-choice as inflating apparent comprehension) [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)] [Category A: [Chan & Ng, CIOP PDF](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)]. A large-scale crowdsourced example: a study recruited 100 US-based Amazon Mechanical Turk workers to guess the meaning of 20 US Pharmacopeial medical pictograms in an open-ended "Guess what the image tells you" task, with two independent coders rating each response on a four-point correctness scale (correct / partially correct / wrong / completely wrong), achieving Krippendorff's alpha of 0.83 intercoder agreement, and computing comprehensibility as percentage of correct-plus-partially-correct guesses — a direct crowdsourced analogue of the ISO 9186-1 procedure [Category A: [Crowdsourcing Participatory Evaluation of Medical Pictograms Using MTurk, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2013/6/e108/)].

### (c) Context-of-use / in-situ testing — "does the symbol work correctly embedded in a real signage system, product, or communication task?"

This construct asks whether the symbol functions correctly as part of an actual task performed in a realistic environment, not just whether it is nameable or guessable in a vacuum. ANSI Z535.3's 2011 revision explicitly recommends testing safety symbols **in context** because presenting a symbol without any pictorial or verbal cue to its real-world placement is "adversely" easier to misread than testing it embedded in its intended label/sign context [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview, ANSI webstore](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)] [Category A: [IIID, Olgyay, "Building on Test Results to Design Safety Symbols" PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Olgyay-Nora--Building-on-Test-Results-to-Design-Safety-Symbols.pdf)], and empirical work on context-free symbol testing documents specific misreadings that occur only without context — e.g., a vehicular "no entry" symbol misread as a "minus sign" or "badge slot," or a laser-hazard symbol misread as a "kitchen sponge," when shown without any signage/product surround [Category A: [IIID, Olgyay, "Building on Test Results to Design Safety Symbols" PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Olgyay-Nora--Building-on-Test-Results-to-Design-Safety-Symbols.pdf)]. Wayfinding research operationalizes this as simulated navigation/task-completion studies: e.g., immersive/virtual-reality pre-occupancy evaluation of wayfinding signage in built environments [Category A: [Pre-occupancy evaluation of wayfinding signage using immersive VR, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0142694X25000420)], EEG-based virtual-response testing of healthcare wayfinding designs where task success (time-to-destination, error rate) is the outcome rather than isolated symbol naming [Category A: [Evaluating Wayfinding Designs in Healthcare Settings through EEG and Virtual Response Testing, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.10.430638v1.full.pdf)], and tactile-signage navigation-task studies with visually impaired participants measuring actual wayfinding performance rather than symbol recognition alone [Category A: [Tactile-Based Signage Design Intervention on Wayfinding Performance of Visually Impaired Students](https://jurnal.unimed.ac.id/2012/index.php/gorga/issue/https:/jurnal.unimed.ac.id:443/2012/index.php/gorga/article/view/62421)]. In AAC, the equivalent is longitudinal device-use research and intervention trials such as PECS efficacy studies, which measure whether a symbol/protocol produces successful real-world communicative outcomes (e.g., increased spontaneous requesting) over weeks or months of actual use, rather than one-shot guessing accuracy [Category A: [Travis & Geiger, PECS South African pilot study, SAGE Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659009349971)]. The Nielsen Norman Group frames this practically as **in-context usability testing / A/B testing**: give users a realistic task, observe whether and how they use the icon to complete it, appropriate once a working prototype exists [Category B: [Nielsen Norman Group, "How to Test Digital Icons"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-test-digital-icons/)].

| Construct | What it measures | Typical method | Example source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognition | Is the visual form nameable/identifiable at all? | Out-of-context naming/description, ISO 9186-2 element naming | [ISO 9186-2:2008](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf) |
| Comprehension | Can the intended abstract meaning be correctly inferred? | Open-ended guessing or MC selection, scored vs. 66%/85%-style thresholds | [ISO 9186-1:2014](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf) |
| Context-of-use | Does the symbol function correctly in a real task/system? | Simulated wayfinding, task-completion studies, longitudinal device-use/intervention trials | [ANSI Z535.3-2011](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf); [PECS pilot study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659009349971) |

## 4. Cross-Cultural Testing Methodology

ISO 9186:1989's own panel requirement is itself a cross-cultural testing design: **at least four countries, at least 100 respondents per country, balanced across three age bands** [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)], and later editions preserve the principle that "testing for comprehensibility [may be] conducted in a minimum number of countries" as set by the adopting standards body, with a strict quality-equivalence requirement across countries: identical material quality, screen presentation quality, legibility, and image quality wherever the test is administered [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)].

Academic studies extend or stress-test this cross-cultural design in two directions. First, controlled comparative studies test the *same* symbol variants across multiple national/linguistic groups to check for consistent versus divergent interpretation, as in Foster's UK/Korea/Iran ATM-symbol study, which found general cross-country agreement on which variant was best but a systematic magnitude difference for Iranian respondents [Category A: [Foster, Applied Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207590444000258)], and the teleconferencing-icon study that explicitly tested Western-designed symbols on Southeast Asian participants to probe cross-cultural transfer failure [Category A: [Chan & Ng, CIOP PDF](https://m.ciop.pl/CIOPPortalWAR/file/72500/201312121025&R1999-V5-N4-str513-528.pdf)]. Second, applied public-health and migration research directly tests standardized (ISO/USP/South African) pictograms on migrant and refugee populations in transit or reception settings — both retrieved studies were conducted at a Temporary Stay Centre for Immigrants in Melilla, Spain, surveying newly arrived sub-Saharan migrants aged 18+ with the USP and South African pictogram sets respectively, and both found **universal failure to meet the 66.70% ISO threshold**, with no significant relationship between comprehension and health literacy or other sociodemographic variables — evidence that the failure reflects a cultural/visual-literacy mismatch in the pictograms themselves rather than participants' general literacy [Category A: [Comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms in sub-Saharan migrants, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38158019/)] [Category A: [Comprehensibility of South African pictograms by sub-Saharan African prospective-users, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37567037/)]. Related pharmaceutical-pictogram research has developed and evaluated pictogram sets purpose-built for low-literacy, culturally diverse, multiethnic populations, treating cultural/literacy adaptation as a design requirement rather than an afterthought [Category A: [Development and evaluation of pictograms for limited-literacy multiethnic populations, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355379/)].

## 5. Practical Recommendations for a Small, Unfunded, Open-Source Project

Full ISO 9186 compliance (four countries, 100+ respondents per country, professional judge-coding with inter-rater reliability statistics) is out of reach for a solo builder or small open-source community project. The methodological literature nonetheless supports a scaled-down, honestly-labeled evidence program:

- **Minimum viable sample sizes.** ISO 9186-2's own perceptual-quality test only requires 25 respondents per condition [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)], and ANSI Z535.3's built-in "Comprehension Equivalencies" table exists precisely to handle "if less than 50 subjects must be used" [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview, ANSI webstore](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)] — both signal that the standards bodies themselves accept smaller-N designs as legitimate when scaled honestly, and n≈20–30 per icon (as used in several AAC and eye-tracking studies cited above) is a defensible minimum for a first-pass signal rather than a certification claim [Category A: [Effects of Pictogram and Typeface Complexity on Visual Attention, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398171/)].
- **Lightweight remote-testing designs.** Adapt the open-ended ISO 9186-1 prompt ("What do you think this symbol means?") into a short online form (Google Forms, Typeform, or a static web survey), randomizing icon order per respondent as ISO recommends, and coding responses afterward with a simple correct/partially-correct/wrong/don't-know rubric rather than the full seven-category ISO scheme.
- **Crowdsourced/online-panel testing.** The MTurk medical-pictogram study is a directly reusable template: recruit ~100 online panelists (Prolific and MTurk both support pre-screening, in Prolific's case via built-in attention/comprehension-check policies) [Category B: [Prolific's Attention and Comprehension Check Policy](https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445153-prolific-s-attention-and-comprehension-check-policy)], pay a small per-task fee, use an open-ended "what does this symbol tell you to do" prompt, and have two people independently code responses to compute basic inter-rater agreement — this is well within a few hundred dollars and a few days for a 15–20-icon lexicon [Category A: [Crowdsourcing Participatory Evaluation of Medical Pictograms Using MTurk, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2013/6/e108/)].
- **Free/open datasets as comparison baselines.** ARASAAC's openly published normative data (name agreement, familiarity, transparency, translucency for 1,525 pictograms) is free to reuse as an external benchmark or as stimuli/methodology templates, letting a small project calibrate its own numbers against a known, previously-validated open AAC symbol set rather than inventing norms from nothing [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency norms](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)] [Category A: [Norms for a Pictographic System: ARASAAC, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305113/)].
- **Focus-group alternatives.** Qualitative, low-cost alternatives to formal ISO focus groups include short structured interviews using the symbols themselves as talking points (a Talking-Mats-style visual interview), or asynchronous feedback threads on GitHub/Discord where community members react to and discuss draft icons — consistent with participatory-AAC-research guidance on accessible, non-verbal-heavy qualitative methods for populations who may find spoken focus groups difficult [Category A: [Qualitative Methods for Involving AAC Users as Primary Research Participants, ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3313831.3376883)].
- **Honest reporting of limitations.** Any published results should explicitly state: sample size and its relationship to ISO/ANSI minimums; that the panel is not multi-country/multi-culture unless it demonstrably is; whether testing was open-ended or multiple-choice (and that the literature considers multiple-choice comprehension scores inflated relative to open-ended scores) [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)]; and that results are a directional signal, not a formal ISO/ANSI certification.

## 7. Five Concrete Experiments Pictiq Could Run

Pictiq is a small open-source compositional visual-language project with a canonical lexicon of roughly 80 ordinary icons plus an entity registry, organized into concepts such as needs, places, money, quantity, movement, safety, and communication, alongside a Paris-travel context pack [Category B: [Pictiq repository, GitHub](https://github.com/markoblogo/pictiq)]. The following five experiments are scaled to a solo-builder/small-community budget, grounded directly in the methodologies reviewed above.

**Experiment 1 — Open-ended comprehension test of the core lexicon (construct: comprehension)**
*Research question:* What percentage of naive respondents correctly infer the intended meaning of each core Pictiq icon when shown alone, with no label or context?
*Design:* Adapt the ISO 9186-1 procedure directly: build a short web survey (Google Forms/Typeform) showing one randomized icon per screen and asking "What do you think this symbol means?"; for icons with an implied action (e.g., safety icons), add the follow-up "What should you do in response to this symbol?" Randomize icon order per respondent as ISO recommends, and cap each respondent's set at 15 icons to avoid fatigue, splitting the ~80-icon lexicon across multiple respondent groups if needed [Category A: [ISO 9186-1:2014, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/59226/10ccdf51569a4aeb87e0b42671a6c0e9/ISO-9186-1-2014.pdf)].
*Minimum sample size:* 25–30 respondents per icon subset (following ISO 9186-2's 25-respondent minimum-group convention rather than the full 100-per-country ISO 9186-1 panel) [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)].
*Scoring:* Two independent coders classify each response as correct / partially correct / wrong / don't-know using a simplified four-category rubric analogous to the MTurk medical-pictogram study, computing percent-correct per icon and reporting inter-rater agreement (e.g., Cohen's kappa) [Category A: [Crowdsourcing Participatory Evaluation of Medical Pictograms Using MTurk, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2013/6/e108/)]. Report results explicitly against — but not as formal certification of — the 66% ISO/ANSI reference points.

**Experiment 2 — Recognition/element-identification pretest for new icon candidates (construct: recognition)**
*Research question:* Are the visual elements composing a new or redesigned Pictiq icon (e.g., a "safety" or "movement" icon) clearly identifiable as discrete, nameable shapes before comprehension testing begins?
*Design:* Following the ISO 9186-2 logic, show each candidate icon to respondents and ask them to name the visual elements they see (e.g., "arrow," "coin," "person"), without asking what the icon as a whole means; this acts as an inexpensive early-stage filter to catch icons whose components are ambiguous or unrecognizable before investing in full comprehension testing [Category A: [ISO 9186-2:2008, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/43484/5c93ca45a3ee4fedb800a46cab13da58/ISO-9186-2-2008.pdf)].
*Minimum sample size:* 15–20 respondents per candidate icon (below ISO 9186-2's formal 25-respondent minimum, but adequate as a triage step given the project's resource constraints; label results explicitly as a pretest, not a validated recognition score).
*Scoring:* Percentage of respondents correctly naming each key visual element; icons where fewer than ~70% of respondents correctly name a load-bearing element are flagged for redesign before comprehension testing.

**Experiment 3 — In-context task-completion test using the Paris travel context pack (construct: context-of-use)**
*Research question:* Do Pictiq's Paris-travel-landmark and movement/places icons actually help a user complete a realistic wayfinding or communication task when embedded in a simulated travel scenario, versus tested in isolation?
*Design:* Recruit participants (ideally including some non-native-English/non-native-French speakers to approximate the traveler population Pictiq targets) and give them a short simulated task, e.g., "Using only these icon tiles, ask someone where the nearest metro station or pharmacy is" or "Build a message asking for help finding your hotel," observing whether they select the intended icons and whether an independent second participant can correctly interpret the composed message — a scaled-down analogue of task-completion wayfinding studies [Category A: [Pre-occupancy evaluation of wayfinding signage using immersive VR, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0142694X25000420)] and of ANSI's recommendation to test symbols in situ rather than in isolation [Category A: [ANSI/NEMA Z535.3-2011 preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/NEMA/preview_ANSI+NEMA+Z535.3-2011.pdf)].
*Minimum sample size:* 10–15 dyads (sender/receiver pairs), which is small but sufficient to surface qualitative failure patterns (e.g., systematic misreadings) rather than to produce a precise percentage; report as exploratory task-based evidence.
*Scoring:* Binary task-success rate (did the receiver correctly identify the sender's intended request?) plus qualitative logging of specific breakdowns (which icon combinations were misread and how), similar to the "critical confusion" logging used in ANSI-style testing [Category A: [IIID, Olgyay, "Building on Test Results to Design Safety Symbols" PDF](https://www.iiid.net/PublicLibrary/Olgyay-Nora--Building-on-Test-Results-to-Design-Safety-Symbols.pdf)].

**Experiment 4 — Cross-cultural/cross-literacy comprehension comparison via free online panels (construct: cross-cultural)**
*Research question:* Do Pictiq's safety, needs, and quantity icons show materially different comprehension rates across language/cultural groups the project explicitly targets (e.g., English speakers vs. a non-English-dominant migrant or refugee-adjacent sample), replicating the design logic of migrant pictogram-comprehension studies at low cost?
*Design:* Run the same open-ended comprehension survey from Experiment 1 twice, recruiting one general-population online sample (e.g., via Prolific, using its built-in comprehension-check screening) [Category B: [Prolific's Attention and Comprehension Check Policy](https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445153-prolific-s-attention-and-comprehension-check-policy)] and one sample explicitly drawn from a different language/cultural background (e.g., partnering with a local language-exchange, refugee-support, or international-student community group for recruitment, mirroring the migrant-reception-center recruitment strategy used in the sub-Saharan migrant pictogram studies) [Category A: [Comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms in sub-Saharan migrants, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38158019/)]. Compare per-icon comprehension rates between groups.
*Minimum sample size:* 20–25 respondents per group (two groups minimum = ~40–50 total), acknowledging this falls well short of ISO's four-country/100-per-country design and must be reported as such [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)].
*Scoring:* Percent-correct per icon per group, with a simple chi-square or Fisher's-exact comparison flagging icons with statistically or practically meaningful cross-group gaps for redesign priority; icons under 66% in either group are flagged regardless of significance, following the ISO safety-critical convention of treating the threshold strictly [Category A: [ISO 9186:1989, iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/16810/f1a635dc9b0a425fbdf210ae527a04eb/ISO-9186-1989.pdf)].

**Experiment 5 — Transparency/translucency norming of the canonical lexicon using an ARASAAC-style protocol (construct: recognition + comprehension, benchmarked)**
*Research question:* What are the transparency (unlabeled naming) and translucency (labeled fit-rating) scores for each Pictiq icon, and how do they compare to published ARASAAC norms for visually similar concepts, giving Pictiq a reusable, benchmarked open dataset rather than a one-off pass/fail result?
*Design:* Directly replicate the two-task ARASAAC norming protocol at small scale: Task A (transparency) shows each icon alone in a simple web app (a lightweight jsPsych-style page is sufficient) and asks respondents to type one word describing its meaning; Task B (translucency) shows the same icon with its intended Pictiq label beneath it and asks respondents to rate the fit on a 7-point scale (1 = no relationship, 7 = very strong relationship), including a handful of deliberately mismatched label/icon pairs as attention checks, following the ARASAAC design [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].
*Minimum sample size:* Given Pictiq's lexicon of roughly 80 icons (versus ARASAAC's 1,525), a single subtask of 30–40 respondents per task is feasible and proportionate, well below ARASAAC's 60–65-per-subtask design but adequate for a first open dataset release [Category A: [Frontiers in Psychology, ARASAAC transparency/translucency study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1467796/full)].
*Scoring:* For transparency, report the proportion of respondents producing the intended/modal name per icon (name-agreement score); for translucency, report the mean 7-point rating and standard deviation per icon; publish the full per-icon dataset openly (mirroring ARASAAC's own open-data ethos) so future contributors and researchers can reuse or extend it rather than re-deriving norms from scratch.
