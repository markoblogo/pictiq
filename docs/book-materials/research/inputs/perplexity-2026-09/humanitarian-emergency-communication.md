Pictographic communication is genuinely useful in humanitarian and emergency settings—but mainly for **immediate, concrete, repeatable, low-inference messages**: basic needs, locations, simple actions, hazards, queues, routes, and visible status. It becomes unsafe when a message determines legal rights, clinical decisions, informed consent, safeguarding, family separation, or individualized treatment; in those cases, pictograms should support—not replace—trained interpreters and qualified professionals.

Pictiq’s realistic role is a constrained visual layer for orientation, first contact, low-literacy support, triage-adjacent status communication, and structured handoff prompts. It should never imply that a compact tile string can substitute for medical assessment, asylum interpretation, or a person’s informed decision.

## **Field realities**

Humanitarian communication operates under conditions that make ordinary interface assumptions unreliable:

* **Multilingualism is not one problem.** People may speak different languages, dialects, literacy varieties, sign languages, or none of the operational languages used by responders. Medical-interpreting guidance recommends asking which language the patient is most comfortable speaking, rather than inferring ability from nationality or appearance.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11121150/)\]  
* **Low literacy is not low intelligence.** Written instructions can fail for people with limited literacy, but pictograms can also be misunderstood; inclusive disaster communication guidance warns that uptake of pictographs has sometimes been premature and insufficiently validated.\[[elrha](https://www.elrha.org/news-blogs/people-with-literacy-challenges-are-left-behind-in-disaster-communication)\]  
* **Stress changes interpretation.** Trauma, pain, fear, fatigue, grief, crowding, sensory overload, and time pressure reduce attention and working memory. The receiver may see a pictogram but fail to infer the intended next step.  
* **Context is unstable.** A symbol understood inside a clinic, reception desk, food-distribution site, or escorted route may become ambiguous on a roadside, in a temporary shelter, or on a phone with no staff nearby.  
* **Cultural conventions differ.** Gestures, colors, household objects, food imagery, family configurations, clothing, gender markers, clocks, maps, and medical symbols may not carry a uniform meaning across regions.  
* **Access needs compound.** Visual materials do not solve communication for blind or low-vision users, people with cognitive disabilities, people in darkness/smoke, people without a device, or people who cannot decode the icon style.  
* **Power asymmetry matters.** A person receiving aid, seeking asylum, or undergoing triage may feel unable to say “I do not understand.” Apparent assent to a pictogram must not be treated as informed consent.  
* **Legal and medical records require fidelity.** In asylum procedures, interpretation guidance emphasizes preserving the speaker’s pronouns, order, dialect boundary, and message content, while limiting the interpreter’s role to interpretation rather than advice. That is fundamentally incompatible with a lossy visual summarization layer as the official record.\[[euaa.europa](https://www.euaa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/2024-02/2024-Practical-Guide-Interpretation-Asylum-Procedure-EN_0.pdf)\]

The design principle is:

> Use visual communication to reduce immediate friction and help people orient themselves; use trained humans, accessible alternatives, and formal procedures whenever meaning affects rights, diagnosis, treatment, consent, protection, or accountability.

## **Existing systems and lessons**

### **Humanitarian icon libraries**

The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, OCHA, released humanitarian icon sets for crisis communication. The 2012 set began with 250 icons across clusters, disaster types, affected-population categories, and relief items; later releases expanded to 500 freely available icons designed to help relief workers communicate emergency and crisis information quickly and simply.\[[unocha](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/world/iconography-part-un-s-humanitarian-efforts-ocha-releases-new-humanitarian-icons)\]\[[unocha](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/world/world-humanitarian-and-country-icons-2012)\]

The 2018 set included 295 icons spanning humanitarian clusters, disasters, logistics, security incidents, and UI-related themes. These are most effective as a **shared professional visual vocabulary** for maps, dashboards, assessments, coordination material, and service signage.\[[reliefweb](https://reliefweb.int/blogpost/humanitarian-icons-20-visual-tools-aid-community)\]

Their existence does not prove that every icon is universally comprehensible to every affected person. It proves that a controlled, reusable icon family helps organizations create consistent visual material.

**Pictiq lesson:** distinguish two audiences:

| Audience | What they need | Pictiq design implication |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Responders and coordinators | Fast, standardized classification, maps, logistics, handoff summaries | Context Pack may use specialist vocabulary and fixed codes |
| Affected people and communities | Immediate, clear, respectful instruction and service orientation | Use only highly tested, concrete, action-focused sequences; provide spoken, textual, and interpersonal alternatives |

A logistics icon such as `WAREHOUSE`, `TRUCK`, or `FUEL` may work well among trained field teams. A displaced family needs an immediately understandable message such as `WATER → HERE`, `FOOD → TIME 14:00`, or `REGISTER → DESK A`, ideally reinforced by staff and local-language voice/text.

### **Emergency risk communication**

IFRC guidance for emergency communication emphasizes that information should be simple, communicate the threat clearly, provide practical action, be accessible in people’s languages and channels, account for literacy, avoid causing harm or panic, and respect local culture and religion.\[[communityengagementhub](https://communityengagementhub.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2019/06/IFRC-CEA-GUIDE-0612-LR-1.pdf)\]

WHO guidance likewise recommends non-technical language in public-health emergencies. Broader emergency-communication research recommends using multiple modes and multiple languages—print, pictorial, audio, internet, television, and interpersonal communication—tailored to local and personal relevance.\[[iris.who](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/259807/9789241550208-eng.pdf?sequence=2)\]\[[aspe.hhs](https://aspe.hhs.gov/reports/analysis-risk-communication-strategies-approaches-risk-populations-enhance-emergency-preparedness-1)\]

The recurring pattern is **redundancy, not replacement**:

Pictogram  
\+ spoken explanation  
\+ local-language text  
\+ trusted community messenger  
\+ physical demonstration  
\+ repeatable route/signage  
\+ accessible alternative

A single Pictiq tile string should therefore be assessed as one communication channel inside a broader response system, not as a self-contained universal language.

### **Emergency triage**

WHO’s Interagency Integrated Triage Tool was developed with WHO, the ICRC, and Médecins Sans Frontières for routine adult/child triage and mass-casualty contexts. It uses a three-colour system: red for high acuity requiring immediate attention, yellow for moderate acuity requiring attention soon, and green for lower acuity where waiting is possible.\[[who](https://www.who.int/tools/triage)\]

Triage categories are operationally useful visual shorthand—but the decision to assign them is made through trained clinical protocols, not by a patient self-selecting a color or by a pictogram alone. Mass-casualty triage similarly prioritizes patients according to clinical urgency; standard descriptions distinguish immediate, delayed, minor, and expectant categories.\[[learn.pedspandemicnetwork](https://learn.pedspandemicnetwork.org/mod/book/view.php?id=1538&chapterid=51)\]

**Pictiq lesson:** represent triage-facing facts and workflow prompts, not diagnosis or final clinical prioritization.

Appropriate:

\[WAIT HERE\]  
\[SHOW ID\]  
\[PAIN\] \[BODY:CHEST\]  
\[BREATHING:DIFFICULT\]  
\[BLOOD\] \[LOSS\]  
\[ALLERGY\]  
\[MEDICINE\]  
\[PREGNANT\]  
\[CHILD\]  
\[HELP\] \[NOW\]

Not appropriate as standalone communication:

\[YOU ARE SAFE\]  
\[YOU HAVE NO EMERGENCY\]  
\[TAKE THIS MEDICINE\]  
\[YOU CAN GO HOME\]  
\[YOUR CONDITION IS MINOR\]

Those statements require professional assessment, context, follow-up instructions, and often interpreters.

### **Pictogram-assisted policing and emergency contact**

A contemporary example is a 46-page emergency communication guide using more than 100 pictograms across 11 scenarios, intended to help police and people communicate when language or disability prevents direct speech. It has reportedly been deployed on devices used by 82,000 officers.\[[zeroproject](https://zeroproject.org/view/project/ed254811-7f52-f011-877b-6045bddd5972)\]

This supports a pragmatic role for pictograms: **rapid question-and-answer scaffolding** during first contact. A responder can point to `HOME`, `CHILD`, `PHONE`, `HURT`, `DANGER`, `TOILET`, `WATER`, or `NEED HELP`, then use gesture, selection, repetition, and escalation to reach a more complete understanding.

It does not remove the need for an interpreter where testimony, consent, criminal allegation, immigration status, safeguarding concern, or medical disclosure is involved.

### **Pharmaceutical pictograms**

Medication pictograms are a warning case. Studies note that poor comprehension of pharmaceutical pictograms can compromise medicine-taking information, particularly for people with low literacy, limited proficiency in the dominant language, older adults, migrants, and refugees.\[[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10623492/)\]

A medication icon can help reinforce a professionally explained regimen:

\[MORNING\] \[TABLET\] \[WATER\]

But it cannot safely carry complex clinical constraints alone:

Take one 250 mg tablet after food twice daily for five days,  
unless rash, breathing difficulty, severe diarrhea, pregnancy concern,  
kidney condition, interaction, or prior allergy requires review.

The visual message may be useful as a reminder. It is not a complete medication instruction or consent process.

## **Suitable pictographic messages**

The strongest candidates have five properties:

1. **Concrete:** refer to visible places, objects, actions, or body states.  
2. **Immediate:** useful now, not dependent on abstract deliberation.  
3. **Low-inference:** a correct action follows directly from the message.  
4. **Reversible:** misunderstanding can be corrected without irreversible harm.  
5. **Testable:** field users can demonstrate understanding, not merely recognize an icon.

| Field function | Suitable Pictiq message types | Why pictograms can work | Required safeguards |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Wayfinding | Toilet, water point, registration desk, clinic, food distribution, exit, charging point, child-friendly space | Concrete destination \+ repeated environmental context | Arrow/direction, distance if relevant, physical staff support |
| Basic needs | Water, food, shelter, blanket, sanitation, charging, hygiene items, baby supplies | Highly recurrent, concrete, often object-based | Make availability and eligibility explicit; avoid promising supply |
| Immediate safety action | Stop, wait, follow, keep distance, wash hands, wear mask, do not drink water, move uphill | Short imperative actions can be demonstrated and reinforced | Test locally; include hazard and action; use multiple modalities |
| Queue and service flow | Wait, line starts here, take number, registration open/closed, return at time, show document | Repeated routine interactions benefit from visual consistency | Exact time/location must be readable in text/numbers too |
| Shelter orientation | Sleeping area, family area, women’s facilities, accessible toilet, reception, lost-and-found, exit | Stable physical layout supports contextual decoding | Avoid identity-based stereotyping; provide accessibility alternatives |
| Basic self-report | Pain location, hunger, thirst, cold, heat, toilet need, separated family member, lost document | Person can point/select rather than formulate a sentence | Treat as prompt for human follow-up, not final assessment |
| Simple status display | Distribution today, clinic open, water unavailable, bus delayed, route closed | Useful when state is observable and periodically updated | Timestamp, source, and update path needed |
| Non-diagnostic symptom prompts | Fever, cough, vomiting, bleeding, breathing difficulty, pain, injury, pregnancy indicator | Can accelerate initial attention and help structure questions | Must lead to clinician/interpreter assessment; no diagnosis from selection |
| Basic communication preference | Speak, write, point, interpreter, sign language, preferred language | Makes communication barriers visible early | Ask rather than infer language or ability |
| Family reunification orientation | Information desk, tracing desk, meeting point, phone/call, child separated | Supports immediate direction and handoff | Do not expose personal/family data publicly |
| Incident reporting prompts | Fire, flood, violence nearby, damaged shelter, no water, blocked route | Useful for rapid alerting and triage of requests | Verify before operational action; protect complainant safety |
| Accessibility routing | Step-free route, quiet space, visual alert, hearing support, wheelchair-accessible toilet | Direct functional need is often more useful than identity label | Confirm individual need; avoid assuming disability from appearance |

A safe visual instruction has a compact structure:

\[HAZARD OR SERVICE\]  
\+  
\[ACTION\]  
\+  
\[PLACE OR DIRECTION\]  
\+  
\[TIME / STATUS IF NEEDED\]

Examples:

\[WATER\] \[NOT SAFE\] \[DO NOT DRINK\]  
\[FOOD\] \[HERE\] \[TIME 14:00\]  
\[CLINIC\] \[OPEN\] \[TODAY\]  
\[FLOOD\] \[GO\] \[HIGH GROUND\] \[NOW\]  
\[REGISTER\] \[DESK B\] \[BRING DOCUMENT\]  
\[TOILET\] \[THIS WAY\] \[ARROW\]

These should still be paired with local-language text and, when possible, human explanation.

## **Messages requiring professionals**

Some messages are unsuitable for pictographic-only communication because the meaning is abstract, high stakes, individualized, legally regulated, clinically contingent, or emotionally sensitive.

| Domain | Why pictograms alone fail | Required human/professional layer |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Clinical diagnosis | Symptoms do not uniquely determine diagnosis; visual selection may be misunderstood | Clinician assessment, examination, testing, interpreter where needed |
| Medication and dosage | Dose, route, timing, contraindications, interactions, consent, adverse effects require precision | Prescriber/pharmacist explanation, written accessible instructions, interpreter |
| Informed consent | Consent requires understanding alternatives, risks, benefits, voluntariness, and questions | Qualified clinician/professional interpreter; documented consent procedure |
| Triage classification | Visual categories are summaries of clinical judgement, not self-service labels | Trained triage professional using validated protocol |
| Mental-health assessment | Distress, suicidal intent, psychosis, trauma, coercion, and risk cannot be inferred safely from icons | Qualified mental-health/protection professional and interpreter |
| Sexual and reproductive health | Consent, pregnancy, assault, confidentiality, and culturally sensitive terms require privacy and accuracy | Trained clinician/case worker, confidential interpretation |
| Gender-based violence and safeguarding | Disclosure can create danger; family/group icons may expose or oversimplify relationships | Specialized protection staff, safe private setting, confidential interpreter |
| Child protection | Guardianship, trafficking, separation, consent, best interests, and identity need formal process | Trained child-protection professionals and legal procedures |
| Asylum claims | Testimony depends on chronology, precise wording, identity, motive, fear, events, and credibility safeguards | Qualified independent interpreter and trained legal/asylum staff |
| Legal rights and detention | A picture cannot adequately explain appeal rights, deadlines, consent, obligations, representation, or consequences | Legal information in understood language and professional interpretation |
| Family tracing or reunification identity | Names, relationships, locations, consent, risk, and verification cannot be safely inferred from icons | Protection protocols, secure records, trained tracing staff |
| Financial assistance eligibility | Criteria, data use, appeal rights, fraud risk, household composition, and payment authorization require clarity | Caseworker support, translated explanations, consent and documentation |
| Security screening or allegations | “Weapon,” “threat,” “suspect,” or “police” images can trigger fear or false accusation | Trained personnel, de-escalation, interpreters, procedural safeguards |
| Evacuation exceptions | Routes may vary by mobility, family members, medical condition, weather, security threat, and changing access | Incident command, local guides, accessible evacuation planning |
| Death notification or missing-person confirmation | Requires dignity, certainty, support, cultural practice, and emotional care | Trained staff, interpreter, psychosocial support |
| Data protection and biometrics | Fingerprint/camera icons do not explain retention, sharing, legal basis, refusal consequences, or consent | Clear translated explanation, privacy officer/caseworker, documented choice |

The rule should be strict:

> If misunderstanding could alter a person’s rights, safety, treatment, family status, liberty, data privacy, eligibility, or legal record, Pictiq can only be a support layer.

Medical guidance for migrant health emphasizes making all possible efforts to ensure effective communication, using short clear sentences through an interpreter, allowing pauses for clarification, and recording interpreted-session details. This is not a task that a lossy pictogram system should attempt to replace.\[[gov](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/language-interpretation-migrant-health-guide)\]

## **Design constraints for Pictiq**

### **Do not confuse recognizability with understanding**

A water-drop symbol may be recognized as water but misunderstood as:

* Drinking water is available.  
* Wash water is available.  
* Water is unsafe.  
* Bring water.  
* Flooding occurred.  
* A hydration service is here.  
* This is the direction to a water point.

Pictiq should test the full intended proposition, not merely whether users name the object correctly.

Weak:  
\[WATER\]

Better:  
\[WATER\] \[HERE\]

Stronger:  
\[DRINKING WATER\] \[HERE\] \[OPEN\] \[TIME 08:00–18:00\]

Safer during contamination:  
\[WATER\] \[NOT SAFE\] \[DO NOT DRINK\]  
\[GET\] \[SAFE WATER\] \[PLACE A\]

### **Do not rely on color alone**

Red, yellow, green, fill state, or a warning shape may reinforce meaning, but cannot be the only channel. WHO triage uses color categories, but its tool is embedded in trained protocols and clinical workflow.\[[who](https://www.who.int/tools/triage)\]

For Pictiq:

Red visual styling  
\+ \[URGENT\]  
\+ \[GO NOW\]  
\+ destination/action  
\+ text/speech equivalent

not simply a red pictogram.

### **Make uncertainty visible**

Humanitarian messages often concern changing or uncertain conditions:

Road may be blocked.  
Water point may close early.  
Weather risk is possible.  
Clinic capacity is full for now.  
Distribution is delayed.

A Pictiq emergency profile needs explicit distinctions for:

CONFIRMED  
LIKELY  
POSSIBLE  
UNKNOWN  
CHANGED  
CANCELLED  
OUTDATED  
CHECK WITH STAFF

Without these, a polished visual sign can falsely imply certainty and authority.

### **Build in graceful escalation**

A person who cannot understand, cannot see, has a different language, or needs to describe something complex needs an obvious route to help:

\[HELP\]  
\[INTERPRETER\]  
\[SPEAK\]  
\[WRITE\]  
\[POINT\]  
\[SIGN LANGUAGE\]  
\[STAFF\]  
\[PRIVATE SPACE\]

This should be a core stress-test feature. A system that works only for users who already understand it is not robust humanitarian communication.

### **Separate public signage from private interaction**

Public-facing Pictiq must avoid exposing sensitive facts:

Unsafe publicly:  
\[HIV\]  
\[SEXUAL VIOLENCE\]  
\[ASYLUM\]  
\[MENTAL HEALTH\]  
\[UNDOCUMENTED\]  
\[TRAFFICKING\]

Better public message:  
\[PRIVATE HELP\] \[HERE\]  
\[SPEAK TO STAFF\]  
\[CONFIDENTIAL\]

Sensitive topic tiles can exist in a private, staff-mediated communication board, but even there they must function as prompts for qualified follow-up rather than complete statements.

## **Proposed stress-test corpus**

This is a research corpus proposal, not a deployment protocol. It should test whether Pictiq helps people complete concrete communication tasks under realistic constraints, and where it becomes ambiguous, lossy, culturally unsafe, or professionally inappropriate.

Each item should exist in at least five forms:

1. **Canonical Pictiq sequence** with stable concept IDs.  
2. **Plain-language reference meaning** in multiple target languages.  
3. **Context card** describing location, actors, urgency, and available services.  
4. **Expected interpretation/action** and acceptable variants.  
5. **Risk annotation**: `CONTEXT-SUFFICIENT`, `INTENTIONAL_OMISSION`, `LOSSY`, `GAP`, or `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY`.

### **Corpus strata**

| Stratum | Goal | Example setting | Expected Pictiq role |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| A. Wayfinding | Find a place or route | Arrival point, camp, station, clinic, shelter | Strong candidate for pictograms |
| B. Basic needs | Request or locate essential goods/services | Water, food, toilet, blankets, charging | Strong candidate with availability/status cues |
| C. Queue/service flow | Complete simple repeated administrative steps | Registration, food distribution, appointment desk | Good candidate if dates/documents are explicit |
| D. Public-health action | Follow immediate, low-complexity protective action | Handwashing, mask use, safe-water warning | Candidate only with local testing and multimodal reinforcement |
| E. Basic symptom prompt | Initiate clinical attention or describe concrete discomfort | Pain, bleeding, cough, breathing difficulty | Support tool; must escalate to clinician |
| F. Triage workflow | Understand waiting, priority flow, and where to go | Triage tent, ambulance point | Support tool; no self-diagnosis |
| G. Protection and privacy | Find confidential help without public disclosure | Private support desk, child-protection desk | Directional support only |
| H. Legal/rights boundary | Test inability to preserve legal nuance | Asylum interview, consent, appeal deadline | Must produce `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY` or `GAP` |
| I. Medical/medication boundary | Test inability to preserve dosage and contraindications | Pharmacy, chronic medication, allergy | Must produce `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY` or `LOSSY` |
| J. Dynamic uncertainty | Communicate changing status, confidence, and source | Road closure, delayed distribution, outbreak notice | Candidate if timestamp/source/uncertainty are explicit |
| K. Accessibility | Ensure alternative communication paths | Blind/low vision, deaf/hard of hearing, cognitive disability, motor constraints | Must include nonvisual and human alternatives |
| L. Cultural ambiguity | Identify symbols that differ by culture or local practice | Food, family, gestures, gendered facilities, medical imagery | Test locally; document nonportable symbols |

### **Test dimensions**

Every corpus item should be tested across the following variables rather than with a homogeneous user group.

| Dimension | Variants to include |
| ----- | ----- |
| Language | At least several locally relevant spoken languages and dialect groups; do not treat “Arabic,” “French,” or “English” as sufficient proxy categories |
| Literacy | Fluent readers, limited literacy, non-readers, numeracy variation |
| Familiarity | First exposure to Pictiq, brief onboarding, repeated use |
| Cultural background | Locally relevant communities; avoid assuming any generic “international” sample represents field populations |
| Age | Children, adults, older adults, where ethically appropriate |
| Disability/access | Blind/low vision, deaf/hard of hearing, cognitive/learning disabilities, motor limitations, communication disabilities |
| Delivery medium | Printed sign, laminated card, phone screen, low-resolution device, dim light, crowded signage, staff-held board |
| Stress/load | Calm comprehension test, time-limited task, distracting/noisy environment simulation, but never trauma-inducing research |
| Context | Clear service setting, ambiguous outdoor setting, changing service availability, missing staff |
| Complexity | One tile, two-tile compound, action sequence, conditional message, date/time/quantity-bearing message |
| Consequence | Low stakes, moderate coordination stakes, simulated high stakes with non-deployment safety guardrails |
| Directionality | Left-to-right, right-to-left, top-down, icon grouping/attachment; test whether layout conventions travel |

### **Outcome measures**

Do not use “did participants like the icons?” as the primary metric. Measure actual communication performance:

* **First interpretation:** What does the participant think the message means before explanation?  
* **Action accuracy:** Can the participant perform the intended next action?  
* **False-action rate:** Does the message lead to a plausible but harmful or incorrect action?  
* **Clarification rate:** Does the participant know when they do not understand and seek help?  
* **Time to comprehension:** How quickly can the person identify the immediate action?  
* **Recall after delay:** Can they remember a safety instruction after 10–30 minutes?  
* **Cultural variance:** Which symbols produce divergent interpretations by group?  
* **Accessibility performance:** Can users access equivalent meaning through text, speech, tactile, signing, or assistive technology?  
* **Confidence calibration:** Are users confidently wrong? This is more dangerous than uncertainty.  
* **Staff interpretability:** Can field workers consistently use the same Pictiq sequence without inventing incompatible variants?  
* **Loss classification:** Does each item honestly map to `CONTEXT-SUFFICIENT`, `INTENTIONAL_OMISSION`, `LOSSY`, `GAP`, or `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY`?  
* **Escalation success:** For complex/sensitive cases, does the person reach a qualified interpreter or professional promptly and privately?

## **Classification rules for Pictiq**

Use a conservative five-level outcome scale for this corpus:

| Classification | Meaning in humanitarian use | Example |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| `PRESERVED_EXPLICITLY` | Pictiq directly communicates the task-critical meaning with low ambiguity | `[WATER] [NOT SAFE] [DO NOT DRINK]` |
| `CONTEXT-SUFFICIENT` | Meaning depends on stable, visible setting and can be acted on safely | `[TOILET] [ARROW]` inside a signed shelter |
| `INTENTIONAL_OMISSION` | Information is deliberately absent because it is irrelevant, private, or inappropriate for public display | `[PRIVATE HELP] [HERE]` without naming a sensitive condition |
| `LOSSY` | Important nuance is missing, but the sequence may still serve as an initial prompt | `[CHILD] [SICK]` without symptom, severity, duration, or age |
| `GAP` | Current Pictiq concepts/grammar cannot carry a necessary distinction safely | Exact legal deadline, uncertainty scope, medication dosage |
| `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY` | Even a complete-looking icon sequence must not be used as a substitute for qualified interpretation or assessment | Consent, asylum testimony, diagnosis, treatment refusal, guardianship |

The final category is important. It prevents the project’s success at compact visual communication from being misapplied where fidelity, informed understanding, confidentiality, and professional judgement are non-negotiable.

## **Bottom line**

Pictiq can be stress-tested productively in humanitarian contexts if it is framed as a **supportive communication layer under field constraints**, not as a universal replacement for language or professional practice.

The highest-value corpus material is concrete and action-oriented:

Find water.  
Do not drink this water.  
Wait here.  
Follow this route.  
Registration opens at 14:00.  
I need an interpreter.  
I have chest pain.  
I am separated from my child.  
I need private help.  
Ask staff.

The red-line corpus material is equally valuable because it defines the project’s limits:

I consent to treatment.  
I understand my asylum rights.  
This child is legally in my care.  
Take this dose of medication.  
Your condition is not serious.  
You are eligible for assistance.  
You may be detained.

The research objective should be to find where Pictiq improves immediate orientation, agency, and communication initiation—and to ensure it clearly signals `LOSSY`, `GAP`, or `PROFESSIONAL-ONLY` whenever visual compression would endanger rights, safety, dignity, or informed understanding.
