# Context Economy in Communication: How Environment Substitutes for Explicit Vocabulary — And What It Means for Pictiq

## Introduction and Core Claim

Across linguistics, semiotics, information theory, human-computer interaction, and emergency management, a convergent finding recurs: when the environment, situation, or shared background already carries part of a message, communicators need less explicit symbolic content to convey the same meaning. This report traces that principle — call it *context economy* — through eight independent research traditions, extracting named theories, primary studies, and applied design guidelines. It closes with a balanced, evidence-grounded assessment of the open-source pictogram protocol [Pictiq](https://github.com/markoblogo/pictiq), evaluating both how the principle supports its design and where its assumptions may not hold for Pictiq's actual users.

## 1. Pragmatics: Grice, Relevance Theory, Deixis, and Common Ground

### 1.1 Grice's Cooperative Principle and conversational maxims (foundational theory)

H. Paul Grice's 1975 lecture "Logic and Conversation," published in *Syntax and Semantics, Vol. 3: Speech Acts*, established that talk exchanges are "characteristically, to some degree at least, cooperative efforts," governed by a Cooperative Principle: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged" ([Grice, "Logic and Conversation," 1975](https://www.sfu.ca/~jeffpell/Cogs300/GriceLogicConvers75.pdf)). Grice organized this into four maxim categories — Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner — and the Quantity maxims are directly about vocabulary economy: "Make your contribution as informative as is required (for the current purposes of the exchange)" and do not make it more informative than required ([Grice, 1975](https://www.sfu.ca/~jeffpell/Cogs300/GriceLogicConvers75.pdf); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Implicature"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/)).

The mechanism that lets speakers say less is **conversational implicature**: a hearer infers meaning "by saying something else," where the implicature "depends on features of the conversational context" and "must be inferred by hearers from contextual evidence" rather than being carried by conventional sentence meaning ([SEP, "Implicature"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/)). In the SEP's canonical example, when Alan asks "Are you going to Paul's party?" and Barb answers "I have to work," Barb implicates she is not attending — but only because the *question context* supplies the missing inferential link; the same sentence uttered after a different question would implicate something else entirely ([SEP, "Implicature"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/)). This is the pragmatic core of context economy: identical, minimal linguistic material yields different, richer meanings depending on what context is doing the interpretive work.

### 1.2 Relevance Theory (Sperber & Wilson): interpretive effort against expected cognitive payoff (foundational theory, cognitive-pragmatic model)

Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson's *Relevance: Communication and Cognition* (1986, 2nd ed. 1995) reframes Grice's maxims as following from a single Principle of Relevance, replacing Grice's "code model" (in which "a communicator encodes her intended message into a signal, which is decoded by the audience using an identical copy of the code") with an "inferential model," in which "a communicator provides evidence of her intention to convey a certain meaning, which is inferred by the audience on the basis of the evidence provided" ([Wilson & Sperber, "Relevance Theory," in *Handbook of Pragmatics*](https://people.bu.edu/bfraser/Relevance%20Theory%20Oriented/Sperber%20&%20Wilson%20-%20RT%20Revisited.pdf); primary monograph: [*Relevance: Communication and Cognition*, Sperber & Wilson](https://monoskop.org/images/e/e6/Sperber_Dan_Wilson_Deirdre_Relevance_Communica_and_Cognition_2nd_edition_1996.pdf)). This is precisely the theoretical machinery behind "leaving more unsaid when shared context is rich": relevance is defined jointly by cognitive *effect* (how much a hearer's mental model is usefully updated) and processing *effort* (how much interpretive work retrieving that effect costs), and hearers automatically pursue the interpretation that yields the greatest effect for the least effort. When background context already makes certain effects highly accessible — i.e., cheap to derive — a speaker can utter far less and still be understood, because "utterances automatically create expectations which guide the hearer towards the speaker's meaning" ([Wilson & Sperber, "Relevance Theory"](https://people.bu.edu/bfraser/Relevance%20Theory%20Oriented/Sperber%20&%20Wilson%20-%20RT%20Revisited.pdf)). This is the single clearest theoretical statement of *why* rich shared context permits smaller explicit vocabularies: the "cost" of an unstated inference falls as its contextual accessibility rises, so speakers rationally externalize less when the environment is already doing interpretive work for free.

### 1.3 Deixis: the clearest linguistic case of context-incomplete meaning (foundational descriptive linguistics)

Deixis is the standard linguistic term for expressions such as "here," "there," "this," "now," and "I" whose reference cannot be resolved from the linguistic system alone and instead requires the immediate situational context — speaker, time, and place of utterance ([Levinson-influenced overview, "Deixis," Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deixis); [Essex Student Journal, "Deixis in Modern Linguistics"](https://publications.essex.ac.uk/esj/article/id/23/download/pdf/)). Unlike ordinary lexical items, deictic terms are, by design, semantically incomplete without an anchoring context — they are the linguistic system's own built-in mechanism for outsourcing part of the message to the situation, letting a two-letter word ("it," "here") stand in for content that would otherwise require a full noun phrase or spatial description. This makes deixis the paradigm case the report's principle predicts: language routinely offloads referential specification onto context rather than encoding it symbolically every time.

### 1.4 Clark's theory of common ground (foundational theory, extensively empirically supported)

Herbert H. Clark's *Using Language* (Cambridge University Press, 1996) formalizes **common ground**: "For two people A and B, it is common ground that p if and only if: (1) A and B have information that some basis b holds; (2) b indicates to A and B that A and B have information that b holds; (3) b indicates to A and B that p" ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 4, "Common Ground," Stanford](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch4.Using%20Language.96.pdf)). Clark argues "common ground, then, is a prerequisite for coordination — for joint actions" and introduces **joint salience**: "the ideal solution to a coordination problem among two or more agents is the solution that is most salient, prominent, or conspicuous with respect to their current common ground" ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 4](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch4.Using%20Language.96.pdf)). In his worked example, two squash partners with mutual knowledge that they play squash together can coordinate on "the squash ball" as a Schelling-point solution without any words at all — shared context does the referential work that an explicit description would otherwise need to do ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 4](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch4.Using%20Language.96.pdf)).

Crucially for the Pictiq analysis below, Clark explicitly grounds common ground in **community membership** and **communal lexicons**: "if Soonja is a Korean speaker, I assume she takes as common ground to Korean speakers all the conventional features of Korean — its phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics," and "word knowledge, properly viewed, divides into what I will call communal lexicons, by which I mean sets of word conventions in individual communities" ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 3, "Foundations," Stanford](http://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch3.Using.language.96.pdf)). He notes that using a specialized term like "fermata" or "RBI" requires first "establishing that we are both music enthusiasts" or "that we are both baseball fans" — economy of expression is licensed only once community membership, not just physical co-presence, is established ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 3](http://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch3.Using.language.96.pdf)). Clark also gives a striking empirical demonstration of community-specific common ground in *Using Language*, Chapter 4: using knowledge of New York landmarks (rather than accent), researchers "distinguished New Yorkers from non-New Yorkers 85 percent of the time after just two postcards," and Columbia University students' identifiability judgments of public figures correlated with actual identifiability at r = .95 ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 4](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch4.Using%20Language.96.pdf)) — evidence that common-ground-based economy is empirically real, but is scoped tightly to specific, verifiable communities, not a generic property of "sharing an environment."

## 2. Semiotics: Peirce's Icon/Index/Symbol and Barthes's Connotation

### 2.1 Peirce's trichotomy (foundational theory, philosophy of signs)

Charles Sanders Peirce's classification of signs — elaborated across his writings from the 1860s onward — distinguishes three sign-object relations. An **icon** is a sign interpreted "in virtue of some shared quality" (e.g., a portrait resembling its subject). A **symbol**'s relation to its object is "an imputed character," interpreted "in virtue of some observed general or conventional connection between sign and object" — words are the standard example. An **index**, critically, is a sign "whose relation to their objects consists in a correspondence in fact," interpreted "in virtue of some brute, existential fact, causal connections say" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Peirce's Theory of Signs"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce-semiotics/)). Peirce's own paradigm example of an index is smoke signifying fire: "an example of a sign whose sign-vehicle uses existential facts is smoke as a sign for fire; the causal relation between the fire and smoke allows the smoke to act as a signifier" ([SEP, "Peirce's Theory of Signs"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce-semiotics/)).

This is the semiotic analogue of context economy: an index requires no arbitrary, fully-specified code at all — it "utilize[s] some existential or physical connection between it and its object" — whereas a symbol requires a conventional code that must be learned in full because it carries no inherent connection to its referent ("the sign-vehicle function[s]" via "convention, habit or law," as with "traffic lights as sign of priority") ([SEP, "Peirce's Theory of Signs"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce-semiotics/)). Indexicality is therefore a second, independent mechanism (distinct from pragmatic inference) by which real-world causal/contextual structure substitutes for symbolic specification: the interpreter reads the connection off the situation itself rather than off a memorized code table.

### 2.2 Barthes: connotation and myth as context-dependent meaning layers (foundational theory, applied semiotics)

Roland Barthes's *Mythologies* ("Myth Today") and *Rhetoric of the Image* extend semiotics to argue that images carry a **denotative** (literal) layer and a **connotative** (culturally coded) layer, where the connotative reading depends on the viewer's cultural repertoire rather than on anything intrinsic to the sign ([Barthes, "Rhetoric of the Image," excerpted](https://williamwolff.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Barthes-Rhetoric-of-the-image-ex.pdf); [overview, "Semiotics for Beginners: Denotation, Connotation and Myth"](http://visual-memory.co.uk/daniel/Documents/S4B/sem06.html)). Barthes's framework is relevant to context economy mainly as a **caution**: connotation only functions efficiently when the audience shares the specific cultural codes the image is designed to trigger. Where Peircean indexicality substitutes physical/causal context for arbitrary code, Barthesian connotation substitutes *cultural* context — and that substitution fails silently (producing a different or absent connotation) for viewers outside the relevant cultural community, a point directly relevant to Pictiq's cross-cultural risk discussed in Section 9.

## 3. Information Theory: Shannon, Conditional Entropy, and Predictability

### 3.1 Shannon's mathematical theory of communication (foundational theory, mathematically formalized)

Claude Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (*Bell System Technical Journal*, 1948) is the origin of the formal claim that context reduces the information — measured in bits — that must be transmitted. Shannon defines **conditional entropy**: "we define the conditional entropy of y, H_x(y) as the average of the entropy of y for each value of x, weighted according to the probability of getting that particular x," and proves the general inequality "the uncertainty of y is never increased by knowledge of x. It will be decreased unless x and y are independent events" ([Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," 1948, §6](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)). In a noisy-channel example, Shannon shows that when a received signal is completely uninformative about the transmitted one, the required bits per symbol is maximal (1 bit/symbol in his binary example), but as soon as context (the received signal) carries some predictive relationship to the message, the extra information that must be supplied — the "equivocation" — falls correspondingly (from 1 bit/symbol toward 0.081 bits/symbol in his worked 1%-error-rate case) ([Shannon, 1948, §12](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)). This is the mathematical bedrock of the general "context reduces required channel capacity" argument: the more that one variable (context) predicts another (message), the fewer additional bits the message itself needs to carry.

### 3.2 Applied example: "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" (empirical study)

Shannon operationalized this directly for natural language in "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" (*Bell System Technical Journal*, 1951), using a guessing-game experiment: a subject sees a text so far and must guess the next letter. On one 129-letter passage, "89 or 69% were guessed correctly" — meaning that on average, given the preceding context, more than two-thirds of English letters carry so little *additional* information that a human predictor already knows them ([Shannon, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English," 1951](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf)). In a repeated-guessing variant on 102 symbols, 79 were correctly identified on the very first guess ([Shannon, 1951](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf)). This is the concrete, quantitative demonstration that predictability from context (here, prior letters/words) directly lowers the entropy — and therefore the coding requirement — of each subsequent symbol; it is the theoretical basis for modern predictive-text systems and context-exploiting compression schemes, which allocate fewer bits to symbols the surrounding context already makes likely.

## 4. HCI and Context-Aware Computing

### 4.1 Ecological Interface Design — Rasmussen & Vicente (foundational theory with applied design framework)

Kim Vicente and Jens Rasmussen's "Ecological Interface Design: Theoretical Foundations" (*IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*, 22(4), 1992, pp. 589–606) proposes a framework "based on the skills, rules, knowledge taxonomy of cognitive control," whose goal is "first, not to force processing to a higher level than the demands of the task require, and second, to support each of the three levels of cognitive control" ([Vicente & Rasmussen, "Ecological Interface Design: Theoretical Foundations," 1992](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158017888/SMC.PDF)). The paper's key theoretical move is Rasmussen's **skills-rules-knowledge (SRK) taxonomy**: "information can be interpreted in three mutually exclusive ways — as signals, signs, or symbols — and the way in which information is interpreted determines which of the three levels of cognitive control is activated" ([Vicente & Rasmussen, 1992](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158017888/SMC.PDF)). EID's central design principle is to represent the **work domain's own constraints** directly in the display — via an "abstraction hierarchy" — so operators can perceive task-relevant relationships (e.g., a process's safe operating envelope) directly through skill- or sign-based perception rather than through symbol-based reasoning that requires explicit training on a labeled instruction set. In effect, EID engineers the display so the operator's *perceptual* environment substitutes for cognitively expensive, explicitly-coded instructions — the same underlying trade-off as context economy in language, applied to control-room interface design.

### 4.2 Dey & Abowd's context-awareness framework (foundational applied-CS framework)

Anind Dey and Gregory Abowd's "Towards a Better Understanding of Context and Context-Awareness" (Georgia Institute of Technology) defines: "context is any information that can be used to characterize the situation of an entity," where "an entity is a person, place, or object that is considered relevant to the interaction between a user and an application" ([Dey & Abowd, "Towards a Better Understanding of Context and Context-Awareness"](http://www.cs.umd.edu/class/spring2018/cmsc818G/files/contextdey.pdf)). They identify four primary context types — **location, identity, time, activity** — that "answer the questions of who, what, when, and where" and "act as indices into other sources of contextual information" ([Dey & Abowd](http://www.cs.umd.edu/class/spring2018/cmsc818G/files/contextdey.pdf)). Their working definition of a context-aware system is one that "uses context to provide relevant information and/or services to the user, where relevancy depends on the user's task" ([Dey & Abowd](http://www.cs.umd.edu/class/spring2018/cmsc818G/files/contextdey.pdf)). This framework underlies ubiquitous computing's central design bet: if a system can automatically sense who/what/when/where, users need not explicitly specify that information via symbolic input (menus, forms, commands) — the ambient sensing pipeline substitutes for explicit symbolic vocabulary, exactly mirroring the linguistic-pragmatic pattern in Section 1.

### 4.3 Gibson's affordances (foundational ecological-psychology theory)

James J. Gibson's *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979) introduces the term **affordance**: "the affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill" ([Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, 1979, Ch. 8, p. 119](https://library.uniq.edu.iq/storage/books/file/The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Approach/1667383098The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Classic%20Edition%20(James%20J.%20Gibson)%20(z-lib.org)%20(1).pdf)). Gibson deliberately frames affordances as neither purely objective nor purely subjective: "an affordance is neither an objective property nor a subjective property; or it is both if you like... it is equally a fact of the environment and a fact of behavior" ([Gibson, 1979, p. 121](https://library.uniq.edu.iq/storage/books/file/The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Approach/1667383098The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Classic%20Edition%20(James%20J.%20Gibson)%20(z-lib.org)%20(1).pdf)). His central hypothesis is that "the composition and layout of surfaces constitute what they afford. If so, to perceive them is to perceive what they afford... the 'values' and 'meanings' of things in the environment can be directly perceived" ([Gibson, 1979, Ch. 3, p. 28](https://library.uniq.edu.iq/storage/books/file/The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Approach/1667383098The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Classic%20Edition%20(James%20J.%20Gibson)%20(z-lib.org)%20(1).pdf)). This is the strongest possible version of context economy: meaning need not be *coded* at all if it is *directly perceivable* in environmental structure (a knee-high, flat, rigid surface simply "affords sitting on" without any symbol needed) ([Gibson, 1979, p. 120](https://library.uniq.edu.iq/storage/books/file/The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Approach/1667383098The%20Ecological%20Approach%20to%20Visual%20Perception%20Classic%20Edition%20(James%20J.%20Gibson)%20(z-lib.org)%20(1).pdf)).

Donald Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things* takes Gibson's ecological concept and applies it to product and interface design, arguing that well-designed objects make their possible uses ("affordances") perceptible through physical form itself, reducing or eliminating the need for explicit instructional labeling — a large door handle that looks pushable needs no "push" sign, because the object's shape already communicates the action ([Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files4/4bb8d08a9b309df7d86e62ec4056ceef.pdf)). Norman's applied contribution is best understood as a design guideline built on Gibson's foundational theory, distinguishing affordances (what an object's structure makes possible) from signifiers (deliberate cues that communicate where an affordance is), which matters because Norman is explicit that when affordances are not perceptible, designers must add symbolic signifiers back in — an important qualifier for any design that leans on "the environment will communicate it."

## 5. Signage and Wayfinding: Placement as Disambiguating Context

Wayfinding design guidelines converge on **placement, not just pictorial content**, as the mechanism that lets a minimal, text-free symbol communicate reliably. Formal principles state: "provide frequent directional cues throughout the space, particularly at decision points along journeys," and "the design of decision points must be logical, rational and obvious to a sighted user, ensuring the directional cues relate directly to a building or landscape space" ([*Wayfinding Design Guidelines*, Ch. 2](http://www.construction-innovation.info/www.construction-innovation.info/indexcfe4.html?action=printfriendly&id=1097)). The Society for Environmental Graphic Design's four-category model of wayfinding elements — identification, orientation, direction, and information/regulation — explicitly separates signs that *identify* a destination (context does most of the work) from purely textual regulation signs, and defines environmental graphic design as "the planning, design, and specifying of graphic elements in the built and natural environment" — i.e., a discipline defined by treating the sign and its surrounding environment as one integrated communicative system ([American Planning Association, *Context-Sensitive Signage Design*](https://planning-org-uploaded-media.s3.amazonaws.com/publication/download_pdf/Context-Sensitive-Signage-Design.pdf)).

This is codified concretely in **ISO 7001** ("Graphical symbols — Public information symbols," latest edition ISO 7001:2023, 177 symbols across seven categories including toilets, accessibility, and transport). ISO 7001 symbols "were created with the goal of being able to stand alone without accompanying text," and their design/testing is governed by the companion standard ISO 22727:2007 ("Creation and design of public information symbols — Requirements"); critically, the symbols function "through their pictorial content together with their placement and location context" — for example, "a restroom pictogram placed at a location within a public facility can identify the relevant toilet or restroom without requiring a written label" precisely because it is placed at a door in a place already understood (via architectural and situational cues) to be a candidate restroom location ([Wikipedia, "ISO 7001"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7001)). This is an applied design guideline backed by an international standards process (strength: high for its intended contexts) rather than a controlled experimental study — but Section 9.2 below shows that even ISO-class symbols, precisely because their comprehension is culturally and experientially conditioned, do not comprehend equally across all populations.

## 6. Gesture: McNeill's Co-Speech and Deictic Gesture Research

David McNeill's *Hand and Mind: What Gestures Reveal about Thought* and *Gesture and Thought* (University of Chicago Press) establish that gesture is not ornamental but constitutive of utterance meaning: "gesture is an integral component of language in this conception, not merely an accompaniment or ornament," and such gestures are "synchronous and co-expressive with speech, not redundant" — occurring in roughly "90% of spoken utterances in descriptive discourse" ([McNeill, *Précis of Gesture and Thought*](https://mcneilllab.uchicago.edu/pdfs/Gesture&Thought.pdf)). McNeill's framework of the **imagery-language dialectic** treats gesture as supplying an imagistic, holistic component of meaning that speech's segmented, linear code cannot carry alone ([McNeill, *Précis of Gesture and Thought*](https://mcneilllab.uchicago.edu/pdfs/Gesture&Thought.pdf)). His taxonomy includes deictic/pointing gestures, iconic gestures, metaphoric gestures (e.g., a "cultural metaphoric gesture" such as the "palm up open hand," which presents a discursive object), and pantomime (a category McNeill explicitly separates because pantomime "does not combine with speech" the way true co-speech gesture does) ([McNeill, *Précis of Gesture and Thought*](https://mcneilllab.uchicago.edu/pdfs/Gesture&Thought.pdf)).

The direct relevance to context economy is that deictic gesture (pointing) allows a speaker to reference a specific object, direction, or location in the shared physical environment with a minimal or even zero-word utterance ("that one," or gesture alone), where a fully decontextualized channel (e.g., a written message read later, in a different location) would need a complete verbal description of the same referent. Related gesture-studies literature on demonstrative forms confirms this integration of pointing with minimal deictic words as a systematic linguistic-gestural resource rather than an occasional aid ([Peeters et al.-type work, "Integrating deictic co-speech gestures in demonstrative forms"](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/server/api/core/bitstreams/d65e7ec1-d7c4-4a33-a4ce-65df841baf5b/content)). This body of work is foundational-theoretical (McNeill's dialectic framework) supplemented by a substantial empirical gesture-studies literature (strength: strong, cross-validated across many experimental studies of speech-gesture synchrony).

## 7. Emergency Communication: Plain Language and Procedural Minimalism

### 7.1 FEMA/NIMS plain-language mandate (applied policy)

The U.S. National Incident Management System (NIMS) mandates plain, common terminology instead of jargon or 10-codes specifically *because* multi-agency emergencies remove the shared professional context that would otherwise let specialized codes work: "it is important that responders and incident managers use common terminology. There simply is little or no room for misunderstanding in an emergency situation" ([FEMA, "NIMS Alert: Plain Language"](https://www.fema.gov/pdf/emergency/nims/plain_lang.pdf)). Plain language is "required for multi-agency, multi-jurisdiction and multi-discipline events, such as major disasters and exercises," and beginning FY2006, "federal preparedness grant funding is contingent on the use of plain language in incidents requiring assistance from responders from other agencies, jurisdictions, and functional disciplines" ([FEMA, NIMS Alert](https://www.fema.gov/pdf/emergency/nims/plain_lang.pdf)). Notably, the policy explicitly *permits* 10-codes for single-agency internal use, where shared training and community membership (in Clark's sense) still hold: "the use of 10-codes in daily operations will not result in the loss of federal preparedness funds" ([FEMA, NIMS Alert](https://www.fema.gov/pdf/emergency/nims/plain_lang.pdf)). This is a critical, non-obvious inversion of the context-economy pattern: coded, minimal vocabularies (10-codes) are efficient exactly because they rely on shared professional context, and are *abandoned* precisely when that shared context can no longer be assumed across responding agencies — i.e., codes shrink vocabulary only within a verified community; across communities, NIMS moves toward *more* explicit, less context-dependent language, not less.

### 7.2 Common Alerting Protocol — structured context substituting for free vocabulary (applied technical standard)

The OASIS Common Alerting Protocol (CAP) v1.2 achieves communicative economy through the opposite technique: rather than relying on ambient shared context, CAP declares a fixed, structured schema of fields. Each alert's `<info>` segment "describes an anticipated or actual event in terms of its urgency (time available to prepare), severity (intensity of impact) and certainty (confidence in the observation or prediction)," using bounded code vocabularies (e.g., `<category>` values limited to Geo, Met, Safety, Security, Rescue, Fire, Health, Env, Transport, Infra, CBRNE, Other) ([OASIS, *Common Alerting Protocol Version 1.2*, §§1.3, 3.2.2](https://docs.oasis-open.org/emergency/cap/v1.2/CAP-v1.2-os.html)). CAP's vocabulary is small not because senders and receivers share ambient real-world context, but because the protocol *manufactures* an explicit, universally-declared context frame (the schema itself) that every implementation must adopt in advance — a distinction highly relevant to the Pictiq assessment in Section 9.

### 7.3 ICAO MAYDAY/distress phraseology (applied procedural standard)

Aviation radiotelephony phraseology is the clearest case of "procedural minimalism": a distress call is prefixed "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY," and must contain a fixed slot set — station addressed, call-sign, nature of emergency, fuel endurance, persons on board, and supporting information (position, level, speed, heading, intentions) — as in the worked example: "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY, Metro Control, Big Jet 345, main electric failure, request immediate landing at Metro, position 35 miles north west of Metro, heading 120 flight level 80 descending, 150 persons on board, endurance three hours" ([ICAO Standard Phraseology, Skybrary](https://skybrary.aero/sites/default/files/bookshelf/115.pdf)). The standard explicitly separates distress ("MAYDAY") from urgency ("PAN-PAN") and states that "phraseology has evolved over time and has been carefully developed to provide maximum clarity and brevity in communications while ensuring that phrases are unambiguous," while conceding that "not every conceivable scenario will be catered for" and that crews "should be prepared to use plain language when necessary" outside the standard cases ([ICAO Standard Phraseology, Skybrary](https://skybrary.aero/sites/default/files/bookshelf/115.pdf)). Here the "context" that licenses the minimal fixed vocabulary is not ambient environmental context but *procedural/institutional* context: universal training and rehearsed protocol create the shared background that makes "MAYDAY" alone (before any content is even given) instantly and unambiguously meaningful worldwide.

## 8. Situated Language and Situated Cognition: Suchman's Foundational Critique

Lucy Suchman's *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human-Machine Communication* (Cambridge University Press, 1987) is the foundational HCI/cognitive-science argument that action (and communication) is structured moment-to-moment by immediate situational and interactional context, not fully specified in advance by symbolic plans ([Suchman, *Plans and Situated Actions*, Cambridge University Press, 1987 — publication record](https://archive.org/details/planssituatedact0000such); companion analysis: [Vera & Simon, "Situated Action: A Symbolic Interpretation"](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/vera-simon.pdf)). Suchman's central thesis — developed from an ethnomethodological study of users struggling with a "smart" photocopier's plan-based help system — is that a plan functions as a *resource* that participants draw on and reinterpret contingently as a situation unfolds, rather than as a *determinant* that fully specifies the action in advance; actors continuously improvise, repair, and locally interpret their circumstances rather than mechanically executing a pre-stored symbolic procedure ([summarized in secondary scholarly treatment, "Re-re-reading Lucy Suchman's Plans and Situated Actions"](https://www.fordes.de/posts/rerereading_suchman_plansActions.html); [Vera & Simon, "Situated Action: A Symbolic Interpretation"](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/vera-simon.pdf)). Suchman's argument generalizes the report's overall thesis to action and cognition broadly: representations (whether plans, instructions, or symbolic vocabularies) are inherently incomplete relative to situated practice, and successful action/communication routinely depends on real-time, in-context interpretation that no finite symbolic specification can fully anticipate. This is a foundational theoretical/ethnomethodological contribution (grounded in detailed observational case study), not a large-N empirical study, but it remains one of the most cited works establishing that context does structural work that plans/symbols cannot fully substitute for.

## 9. Application to Pictiq: A Balanced, Evidence-Grounded Assessment

[Pictiq](https://github.com/markoblogo/pictiq) is described in its own repository as "a minimal visual protocol for short messages across language barriers," explicitly "designed for pointing, quick signs, stickers, portable communication surfaces, and short composable phrases," and explicitly "not intended to replace natural language or long-form writing" ([Pictiq README](https://github.com/markoblogo/pictiq)). Its canonical registry currently holds 83 ordinary icons, drawn down to a smaller "Core Vocabulary," with concepts like `need_water`, `need_food`, `money_coins`, and `place_home` in its `universal-core` pack, and separate "Context Packs" such as `city-paris-v0.1` (Eiffel Tower, Arc de Triomphe, Louvre pyramid, croissant, etc.) and `road-wayfinding-v0.1` (directional operators, `move_car`, `safety_medical`, `need_food`, `state_dead`) layered on top ([Pictiq `packs/universal-core.json`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/markoblogo/pictiq/main/packs/universal-core.json); [Pictiq `packs/city-paris-v0.1.json`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/markoblogo/pictiq/main/packs/city-paris-v0.1.json); [Pictiq `packs/road-wayfinding-v0.1.json`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/markoblogo/pictiq/main/packs/road-wayfinding-v0.1.json)). Pictiq's own protocol specification explicitly and self-consciously invokes the context-economy principle this report has traced, distinguishing "Embodied Communication" (live exchange where "live pointing, gaze, gesture, voice, visible objects, and shared context can make a tile unnecessary") from "Standalone Communication" (durable artifacts like signs or cards that "must preserve the same meaning after the communicator is gone"), and stating the design rule directly: "design only what the communication surface cannot already provide" ([Pictiq `spec/PROTOCOL.md`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/markoblogo/pictiq/main/spec/PROTOCOL.md)).

### 9.1 Supporting evidence

Several strands of the research reviewed above give real, non-trivial support to Pictiq's architecture:

- **Relevance theory's effort/effect calculus directly justifies the Embodied/Standalone split.** Sperber and Wilson's principle that hearers pursue maximal cognitive effect for minimal processing effort predicts exactly what Pictiq's protocol states: when gaze, pointing, or a visible referent already supplies an effect cheaply, adding an explicit tile increases effort with no compensating gain, so the rational design choice is to omit it ([Wilson & Sperber, "Relevance Theory"](https://people.bu.edu/bfraser/Relevance%20Theory%20Oriented/Sperber%20&%20Wilson%20-%20RT%20Revisited.pdf)). Pictiq's own "zero-intent" rule (pointing at a tile is a valid message by default) is a direct, if informal, application of this logic ([Pictiq README](https://github.com/markoblogo/pictiq)).
- **Deixis and McNeill's gesture research validate the Embodied mode specifically.** The linguistic fact that "here/this/now" are structurally incomplete without situational anchoring, and McNeill's finding that ~90% of descriptive utterances co-occur with meaning-bearing gesture, both support Pictiq's claim that pointing, gaze, and shared physical presence can legitimately substitute for an explicit tile in face-to-face use ([Wikipedia, "Deixis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deixis); [McNeill, *Précis of Gesture and Thought*](https://mcneilllab.uchicago.edu/pdfs/Gesture&Thought.pdf)).
- **Ecological interface design and Gibsonian affordance theory validate context packs as domain-scoped, not universal, vocabulary reduction.** EID's core principle — represent the *work domain's* actual constraints rather than forcing generic symbolic processing — matches Pictiq's architecture of scoping specialized vocabulary (Paris landmarks, road-safety terms) to the specific domain pack rather than bloating a universal core ([Vicente & Rasmussen, 1992](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158017888/SMC.PDF)). This is a legitimate engineering pattern: Pictiq's own principle that "complexity belongs to the context that requires it, not to Core" is structurally the same move EID makes for control-room displays ([Pictiq `spec/PROTOCOL.md`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/markoblogo/pictiq/main/spec/PROTOCOL.md)).
- **ISO 7001's placement-dependent design is a genuine applied precedent** for symbols (like Pictiq's pictograms) that are engineered to be interpretable partly because of where/when they are deployed, not purely from their pictorial content alone ([Wikipedia, "ISO 7001"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7001)).
- **Shannon's conditional entropy result licenses the general logic** that predictable, context-constrained messages need fewer explicit symbols — Pictiq's compositional grammar (tile + `punct_question` + `logic_no`, etc.) is analogous to a small alphabet whose combinatorics, in a constrained domain, cover much more meaning than the raw tile count suggests, similar in spirit (though far less rigorously quantified) to how English letter redundancy lets predictors guess ~69% of subsequent letters correctly given context ([Shannon, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English," 1951](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf)).

### 9.2 Challenging evidence

The theories most central to justifying context economy — Clark's common ground and relevance theory — are also the ones whose core assumption is most exposed by Pictiq's actual target populations, and the empirical record on cross-cultural symbol comprehension gives concrete reason for concern rather than abstract worry.

- **Clark's framework is explicit that common ground is a property of *verified, specific* communities, not of physical co-presence or a declared "context pack."** Clark's own definition requires that both parties "have information that some basis b holds" and that this basis "indicates to A and B that A and B have information that b holds" — a genuinely mutual, bidirectionally verified state ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 4](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch4.Using%20Language.96.pdf)). His communal-lexicon argument is stated as a warning, not an enabling condition: using a specialized term requires "establishing that we are both music enthusiasts" or "that we are both baseball fans" *first* — community membership must be actively established, not assumed ([Clark, *Using Language*, Ch. 3](http://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Using%20language/Clark.Ch3.Using.language.96.pdf)). Pictiq's target populations — tourists, migrants, AAC users, people from different cultural/linguistic backgrounds — are, by definition, populations where this establishment step is precisely what *cannot* be taken for granted. A Paris context pack pictogram of a "croissant" or a nightlife-domain icon for a culturally specific practice assumes the user already possesses the cultural schema to connect the icon to its intended referent — the very "communal lexicon" membership Clark identifies as a precondition, not a byproduct, of economical reference.
- **Relevance theory's inferential model also presumes a shared cognitive environment, and this is where cross-cultural application is theoretically weakest.** Sperber and Wilson's framework predicts correct inference only when the hearer's "mutual cognitive environment" overlaps substantially with the speaker's; when it does not, the same minimal stimulus either fails to trigger any relevant inference or triggers the wrong one — the theory itself, applied honestly, predicts *comprehension failure*, not economical success, once shared background cannot be assumed ([Wilson & Sperber, "Relevance Theory"](https://people.bu.edu/bfraser/Relevance%20Theory%20Oriented/Sperber%20&%20Wilson%20-%20RT%20Revisited.pdf)). Recent applied work extending relevance theory to intercultural communication and to atypical pragmatic populations (e.g., reframing autistic pragmatic differences through relevance theory) underscores that the effort/effect calculus is itself sensitive to whether interlocutors share inferential defaults — a non-guaranteed condition for AAC users or people with different cognitive profiles ([Cambridge, "Relevance Theory and Intercultural Communication"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-intercultural-pragmatics/relevance-theory-and-intercultural-communication/EB9D62B43748366C2B90530FBB7741A1); [PMC, "Reframing Autistic Pragmatic 'Impairments' Using Relevance Theory"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8117104/)).
- **Directly on-point empirical evidence: cross-cultural pictogram comprehension studies show large, sometimes catastrophic, comprehension gaps even for ISO-standardized symbols.** In Vigoroso et al.'s field study of ISO 11684:1995 agricultural-safety pictograms, Pakistani migrant farmworkers in Italy correctly interpreted pictograms at rates of 0% to 44.8% across four standardized symbols, versus 84.1%–94.2% for Italian farmworkers and 57.7%–89.9% for Pennsylvanian farmworkers on the same symbols — with **zero of 29 migrant participants** interpreting all four correctly, far below the ANSI Z535.3:2011 criterion of 85% correct comprehension required for a symbol to be considered acceptable ([Vigoroso et al., "Comprehension of Safety Pictograms Affixed to Agricultural Machinery," cited via IRIS UniTo](https://iris.unito.it/retrieve/e27ce42f-4956-2581-e053-d805fe0acbaa/Comprehension%20af%20safety-Vigoroso%202019_Agromedicine_revised.pdf)). A separate cross-cultural study of small graphical telecom symbols between Swedish and U.S. participants likewise found "variations in rating patterns that may be culturally influenced," with Swedish participants systematically more conservative in their symbol ratings than American participants, even between two culturally proximate, high-literacy Western populations ([ScienceDirect, "Understanding small graphical symbols: a cross-cultural study"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169814101000075)). If standardized, professionally tested pictograms fail this badly for populations who lack the assumed background schema, the risk is at least as large — arguably larger, given less design and testing rigor — for a smaller, newer, less exhaustively validated protocol like Pictiq being used by exactly the mismatched populations (tourists, migrants, AAC users) it targets.
- **The AAC literature confirms the underlying design risk is not hypothetical.** A scoping study of high-tech AAC device manufacturers found that "little is known about how well high-tech augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems accommodate language variation to meet the needs of people and families who are culturally and linguistically diverse," and concluded that "device manufacturers have just begun to address the needs of families and users who are culturally and linguistically diverse" ([PubMed, "Multicultural considerations in augmentative and alternative communication"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35943447/)). This is precisely the population overlap Pictiq claims (its own repository topics include "aac" and "accessibility"), and the literature's verdict is that the field generally has *not yet solved* the cultural-generalizability problem that context-economy designs implicitly assume away.
- **NIMS's own policy history is an instructive counter-example inside the report's own emergency-communication section.** NIMS mandates *more* explicit plain language specifically when a shared professional context (single-department 10-codes) can no longer be assumed across multiple agencies — the exact inverse move from what "rely on context to shrink vocabulary" would suggest, precisely because emergency management has already learned, operationally, that assumed shared context is a safety liability once the population using the code is heterogeneous ([FEMA, NIMS Alert](https://www.fema.gov/pdf/emergency/nims/plain_lang.pdf)). This is a strong internal precedent for treating "context will disambiguate it" with real skepticism whenever the audience is not a verified, homogeneous group.

### 9.3 A sharper framing: "manufactured context frame" versus "ambient real context"

The evidence above supports a more precise conceptual distinction than "context reduces vocabulary, so Pictiq's design is validated." Two of the report's context-economy mechanisms are qualitatively different:

1. **Ambient, situationally-supplied context** — Clark's common ground built through mutual, verified interaction; McNeill's co-speech gesture; Sperber and Wilson's mutually manifest cognitive environment; deixis anchored to a live speech situation. This form of context economy depends on real-time, bidirectional, verifiable shared knowledge between the specific interlocutors present, and Pictiq's "Embodied Communication" mode (pointing, gaze, shared physical presence) genuinely qualifies as this type — the theoretical support here is solid.
2. **Manufactured/declared context frames** — CAP's structured XML schema, ICAO's rehearsed procedural phraseology, ISO 7001's placement convention, and Rasmussen/Vicente's abstraction hierarchy. These achieve vocabulary economy not by relying on ambient shared background between two specific people, but by *pre-declaring* a fixed, universally-trained or standards-body-validated frame that all parties are expected to have separately learned in advance.

Pictiq's context packs are structurally much closer to category (2) than to category (1), despite the intuitive framing of "the environment supplies missing context." A Paris-travel pack or a domain-specific pack is not context that a tourist and a Parisian shopkeeper *share* by virtue of the situation; it is a fixed vocabulary module the *protocol designer* declares in advance, which each user must separately learn or be taught, much like CAP's schema or ICAO's phraseology require advance training rather than spontaneous ambient inference. That is not necessarily a design flaw — CAP and ICAO phraseology are highly successful precisely *because* they are pre-declared, trained, standardized frames rather than because they rely on improvised shared context — but it means the honest justification for Pictiq's context packs is closer to "we are building a small trained protocol, analogous to aviation phraseology or CAP's schema" than to "the ambient environment will disambiguate this for anyone present." The former is a defensible, evidence-backed design pattern with real precedent; the latter is the theoretically riskier claim, and it is the one most exposed by the cross-cultural pictogram-comprehension data in Section 9.2, precisely because Pictiq (unlike CAP or ICAO phraseology) has no equivalent mandatory training pipeline ensuring every user has learned the declared frame before encountering it in the field.

### 9.4 Verdict

The context-economy principle offers real, well-founded support for Pictiq's *Embodied* mode and for the general architectural decision to keep a small Core vocabulary supplemented by domain-scoped packs rather than one sprawling universal lexicon — this mirrors legitimate, independently validated patterns from ecological interface design, ISO signage placement conventions, and relevance-theoretic effort/effect reasoning. It offers much weaker support for the specific claim that context packs let Pictiq safely use *fewer or broader* symbols for its *Standalone*, cross-cultural, AAC-relevant use cases, because the theories invoked (Clark's common ground, relevance theory) explicitly require verified, shared background knowledge that tourists, migrants, and culturally diverse AAC users are, by the nature of these populations, least likely to already possess — and the empirical cross-cultural pictogram literature (0%–44.8% comprehension for culturally mismatched users on standardized safety symbols, versus 85%+ for matched populations) shows this is not a theoretical nitpick but a documented, large-magnitude failure mode. The more defensible framing for Pictiq's own documentation and future validation work is to treat context packs explicitly as trained, declared vocabulary modules — closer in kind to CAP's schema or ICAO phraseology, which succeed because of systematic training and standardization, not ambient inference — and to prioritize empirical comprehension testing analogous to ANSI Z535.3/ISO 22727 symbol validation specifically with its stated target populations (tourists, migrants, AAC users) before treating "context will disambiguate this" as a safe design default.
