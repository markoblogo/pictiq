# Compositional Grammar vs. Flat Symbol Lookup in Visual and Pictographic Communication Systems

## Framing the question

Every visual communication system built from discrete graphical units faces the same design choice: does each symbol carry one fixed, pre-assigned meaning that a designer must enumerate in advance (a **flat dictionary**, or "symbol lookup"), or can symbols be combined under explicit rules to generate meanings nobody pre-drew (a **compositional grammar**)? Linguists analyzing visual programming languages frame this as the split between a system's **vocabulary** (its lexical inventory) and its **grammar** (the rules for combining that inventory), and the same lens applies cleanly to pictographic and iconic communication systems outside programming ([DeepScratch: Scratch Programming Language paper](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume11No7/Paper_77-DeepScratch_Scratch_Programming_Language.pdf)).

This report examines nine categories of visual/pictographic systems against that lens, evaluating each on seven dimensions: vocabulary structure, compositional rules, documented syntax/grammar, context dependence, extensibility, machine-readability, and whether symbol meaning is atomic or built compositionally. It closes with a direct, evidence-based verdict on whether "most pictogram systems are flat dictionaries" is a defensible generalization, and what a small modern project can honestly claim about its own grammar.

## 1. Blissymbolics (Charles K. Bliss, 1949–present)

Blissymbolics is the strongest historical counter-example to flat symbol lookup in this review. Charles K. Bliss designed it as *Semantography*, publishing full editions in 1949, 1965, and 1978, after developing the idea while a refugee in Shanghai and observing Chinese characters ([Wikipedia: Charles K. Bliss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_K._Bliss); [National Library of Australia finding aid](https://nla.gov.au/nla.obj-291526999/findingaid)). It was explicitly conceived as a generative semantic system, not a picture dictionary.

**Compositional rules and grammar.** Blissymbolics defines actual word-order syntax: subject precedes verb, "not" precedes the verb, questions open with a question indicator, commands open with an exclamation indicator, and place/time expressions open the sentence ([The Structure of Blissymbolics, Uppsala handout](https://media.medfarm.uu.se/play/attachmentfile/video/7849/The_structure_of_Blissymbolics,_handouts.pdf); [Evertype Bliss rules](https://www.evertype.com/standards/by/bliss-rules-20090126.pdf)). Grammatical meaning is carried by small quarter-sized **indicators** placed above base characters: an action indicator marks verbs, a descriptive indicator marks adjectives/adverbs (further distinguishing "before-fact" states like "breakable" from "after-fact" states like "broken"), a thing indicator marks nouns, dedicated indicators mark past/present/future/conditional tense, passive voice, and plurality, and comparative/superlative forms are built by prefixing "more"/"most." Multi-character words follow a **classifier + specifier** structure, where the first character sets the semantic category and subsequent characters narrow it — directly analogous to compounding in natural language morphology. When a needed word is missing, Bliss's own rules prescribe explicit repair strategies: substitute a synonym, change word class, swap the classifier, modify the specifier, add a modifier, or combine existing characters into a new compound ([Evertype rules](https://www.evertype.com/standards/by/bliss-rules-20090126.pdf)).

**Vocabulary and governance.** The system builds outward from roughly 900 basic Semantography-derived characters and about 120 "Key" Blissymbol characters capable of generating the full character set through combination. Blissymbolics Communication International (BCI), the exclusive worldwide licensee since 1975/1982 agreements with Bliss, maintains a growing **Authorized Vocabulary**: 1,400 symbols (1982), 2,500 (1992), 3,588 (2006), and 6,556 as of a July 2026 release ([BCI](https://www.blissymbolics.org/); [BCI vocabulary page](https://www.blissymbolics.org/index.php/component/content/article?id=10&Itemid=0)). BCI itself describes Blissymbolics as "a generative language that allows its users to create new Bliss-words as needed" — an explicit, self-conscious claim to compositional generativity that predates any modern pictogram project by over seven decades.

**Machine-readability.** Blissymbolics has formal digital encoding: ISO-IR 169 (a 1993 ISO/IEC 2375 lexicon-based character encoding), an assigned ISO 639-2/639-3 language code (`zbl`), an ISO 15924 script code (`Blis`, 550), and an active 2023 Unicode Consortium proposal (L2/23-138) to encode 1,463 characters at the character level for full text-processing support ([Unicode Blissymbolics proposal](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2023/23138-n5228-blissymbols.pdf); [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)). Content is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0 or under a proprietary option.

**Verdict on this system:** compositional grammar, not flat lookup — documented word order, morphology, tense, plurality, and generative compounding, all specified since 1949 and actively governed today.

## 2. ISOTYPE (Otto Neurath, Marie Neurath, Gerd Arntz, 1925–1940s)

ISOTYPE ("International System Of TYpographic Picture Education," originally the "Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics") was developed at Vienna's Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum by Otto Neurath with artist Gerd Arntz (hired 1928) and "transformer" Marie Reidemeister (later Neurath) ([Wikipedia: Isotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language))). It is best understood as a **statistical display convention**, not a language.

**Compositional rules.** Müller's (1991) codification lists nine semiotic rules governing ISOTYPE design, including that symbols must be self-evident and combinable, that a symbol can stand for a set of things as a graphic unit, and that combinations of symbols can form a larger "unit of information" resembling a short narrative ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Neurath, Visual Education](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neurath/visual-education.html)). Its signature convention is **repetition for quantity**: increases in a quantity are shown by repeating a fixed-size pictogram rather than enlarging it, because enlarging introduces ambiguity about whether height or area is being compared. Critically, each repeated pictogram's value is fixed only **within a given chart**, not universally across the system — there is no standing numeral-like grammar analogous to Blissymbolics' plural or tense indicators.

**Explicit limitation, stated by scholars and by Neurath himself.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states plainly that "the pictorial language is semantically, syntactically and pragmatically limited and underdeveloped." Transmission of ISOTYPE depended on trained "transformers" working from exemplars rather than an explicit combinatorial theory, and translation rules between verbal and iconic content were not univocal. Neurath himself called ISOTYPE a "helping language" and concluded it could never become a fully developed language in its own right — a "language-like technique" rather than a language ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neurath/visual-education.html)).

No standing vocabulary count, formal extensibility procedure, or machine-readable encoding is documented for ISOTYPE, consistent with its origin in 1920s–1930s print design predating computing. Its institutional legacy continued through the International Foundation for Visual Education, Isotype Institute Ltd. (chaired by philosopher Susan Stebbing), and the Otto and Marie Neurath Isotype Collection at the University of Reading.

**Verdict on this system:** closer to flat/ad hoc than to grammar — genuinely combinable at the level of chart layout and repetition, but explicitly and self-consciously "syntactically limited," with no morphology, word order, or formal rule system comparable to a language.

## 3. AAC symbol systems — PCS/Boardmaker, ARASAAC, PECS, PODD

Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) systems are the most heterogeneous category in this review: the raw symbol *sets* are largely flat lexical vocabularies, while the *display/organization systems* built on top of them frequently impose genuine grammatical structure.

**PCS (Picture Communication Symbols / Boardmaker):** roughly 4,500+ symbols in the Boardmaker v6 library, mostly one symbol per word-sense, though polysemous words like "PLAY" can have several distinct pictographs. This is fundamentally a **labeling vocabulary** — grammar is imposed externally by how a communication board or app arranges the symbols, not by rules for combining the icons themselves.

**ARASAAC:** an open-source (CC-BY-NC-SA), Government of Aragon-funded pictogram set of roughly 5,000–8,000 symbols organized taxonomically by grammatical category (nouns, verbs, adjectives, social content) and linked to WordNet synsets for about 800 pictograms ([ARASAAC](https://arasaac.org/); [Global Symbols: ARASAAC](https://globalsymbols.com/symbolsets/arasaac/)). Category tagging is a **labeling convenience**, not a generative combination rule — ARASAAC itself does not define how to combine pictograms into novel compound meanings.

**PODD (Pragmatic Organisation Dynamic Display, built on PCS):** the clearest counter-example within AAC. PODD defines pragmatic "branch starters" ("I WANT…", "SOMETHING'S WRONG…"), a core vocabulary of roughly 200 words covering 75–80% of typical speech versus a larger fringe vocabulary, explicit tense cues ("it's already happened" / "about now" / "going to happen" / "always happening"), left-to-right English word-order page layout, and encouraged multi-symbol composition (e.g., combining FRUIT + SALAD rather than storing one dedicated icon) ([PODD Instruction Manual](https://www.scribd.com/document/642569614/Instruction-Manual-for-PODD-communication-books-Printable-book-AA-pdf)). Its aided-language navigation sequences — e.g., "(categories → people → mum)(categories → action → go)(categories → places → home)" for "MUM GO HOME" — function as a genuine, if manually executed, phrase-structure grammar layered on top of a flat icon set.

**PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System):** structured in phases, with Phase IV introducing an explicit **sentence-strip syntax**: an "I want" icon plus an item icon assembled on a detachable strip to form a minimal two-to-three-word sentence, extended in later phases with attributes and comments ([PECS official site](https://pecs.com/picture-exchange-communication-system-pecs/)).

**Academic evidence on grammatical marking.** A 2022 ASHA scoping review of 22 AAC studies found that 13 (59%) reported some morphological or syntactic feature in the symbol system studied, with parts-of-speech marking the most common feature (9 studies) and 7 studies documenting explicit sentence rules or syntax ([ASHA scoping review](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2022_AJSLP-21-00396)). Specific research systems went further: Bhattacharya & Basu built a tense/aspect/mood/word-order icon-to-sentence generator; García-Méndez et al.'s PictoDroid Lite implements full morphological and syntactic lexicons with natural-language generation; Hunnicutt (1984) built Bliss-to-speech synthesis using "Bliss syntax"; Vaillant (1998) built syntactic-tree/taxeme-based icon generation. An earlier PubMed study (Sutton & Gallagher, 1993) found AAC users could learn novel affixation strategies for past-tense marking using four-digit codes ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8114489/)). A Korean study found that part-of-speech category (noun/verb/adverb) significantly affects symbol-recognition response time, evidence that grammatical category is cognitively real to AAC symbol users even where it is not exploited compositionally ([e-csd.org](https://www.e-csd.org/upload/csd-22-3-597.pdf)).

**Verdict on this system:** mixed. Commercial symbol sets (PCS, ARASAAC, SymbolStix) are functionally flat/lexical vocabularies; the display and pragmatic layers built atop them (PODD, PECS sentence strips) and several research prototypes impose real word-order and morphological structure. Grammar in AAC lives predominantly in board/software organization, not in the icon-composition layer itself.

## 4. SignWriting (Valerie Sutton, 1974–present)

SignWriting is a genuinely two-dimensional compositional writing system for sign languages, invented by Valerie Sutton in 1974, adapted from her 1966 DanceWriting notation ([Wikipedia: SignWriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SignWriting)).

**Compositional structure.** The International SignWriting Alphabet (ISWA) defines 652 symbols across seven classes — Hands, Movement, Dynamics, Head/Faces, Body, Punctuation, and Detailed Location ([ISWA Manual, Chapter 1](https://www.signwriting.org/archive/docs6/sw0531_ISWAManual_Chapter1.pdf)). Of 261 hand-symbol variants (built from 10 base hand shapes), American Sign Language alone uses 83. Symbols are arranged spatially within a variable-size "sign box": hand symbols are filled to indicate palm orientation, and movement arrowheads are filled to indicate which hand performs the motion. This is true spatial (not linear) composition — meaning is built by 2D arrangement of independently meaningful graphical primitives, used to write more than 40 countries' sign languages plus non-linguistic gesture.

**Machine-readability.** SignWriting was added to Unicode in 2015 at range U+1D800–U+1DAAF, but Unicode support remains incomplete because the Unicode Consortium's linear text model was not designed for two-dimensionally arranged writing systems; a further proposal for full 2D support is pending ([Wikipedia: SignWriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SignWriting)). "Formal SignWriting" underlies the open-source JavaScript tools SignPuddle (cloud dictionary/document editor) and SignMaker (offline editor), both MIT-licensed, with fonts under the SIL Open Font License and Google-distributed Noto Sans SignWriting support. Two distinct ordering conventions exist: a manual Sign Spelling Sequence for writing order within a sign, and an automatic Sign Symbol Sequence for dictionary sort order.

**Governance.** Unlike Blissymbolics (BCI) or military symbology (NATO), no single governing body was identified for ongoing ISWA maintenance, which may partly explain the incomplete Unicode encoding.

**Verdict on this system:** strongly compositional — arguably the purest example of spatial (as opposed to linear) compositional grammar in this review, since meaning is literally assembled from independently meaningful graphical components positioned in 2D space.

## 5. Emoji

Emoji present the most important nuance in this review, because casual observation and rigorous Unicode specification diverge sharply.

**The formal Unicode grammar is real but narrow.** Unicode Technical Standard #51 defines a formal BNF-style grammar with productions such as `emoji_core_sequence`, `emoji_zwj_sequence := emoji_zwj_element (ZWJ emoji_zwj_element)+`, `emoji_modifier_sequence := emoji_modifier_base emoji_modifier`, `emoji_flag_sequence := regional_indicator regional_indicator`, plus tag and keycap sequence productions ([Unicode UTS #51](https://unicode.org/reports/tr51/)). This grammar is genuinely machine-parseable and governs, for example, how a base person emoji plus a Zero-Width Joiner plus a profession glyph forms "woman firefighter," or how a base emoji plus one of five Fitzpatrick-scale skin-tone modifiers forms a tone variant, or how two regional-indicator letters form a flag. Crucially, **this formal grammar governs composition only within a single emoji grapheme cluster.** It does not define any ordering or combination rule for multiple separate, standalone emoji placed next to each other in a message — that is, Unicode itself specifies no grammar for the sequence "🎉😂🎂" the way it specifies the internal structure of "👩🏽‍🚒".

**Emergent, non-formal patterns exist in usage.** A corpus study of Reddit/Pushshift data (Latent Scholar) proposes an "Emoji Construction Grammar" describing statistically real but non-categorical patterns: faces and hearts tend to close a sequence; certain orderings are preferred (object/activity before affect, e.g., 🎉😂; face before gesture, e.g., 😭🙏); repetition (🔥🔥🔥) intensifies; symmetric framing (✨…✨) marks emphasis; and linker constructions (A➡️B) suggest sequence or causality ([Latent Scholar: From Decoration to Syntax](https://latentscholar.org/from-decoration-to-syntax-emoji-sequences-as-emerging-grammatical-structures-in-digital-communication/)). The paper explicitly distinguishes Unicode-level "hard constraints" (machine-enforced) from community-level "soft constraints" (conventional, not enforced) and "interpretive constraints" (context-dependent), concluding that under a narrow definition of syntax — hierarchical phrase structure, categorical parts-of-speech, recursive embedding — free-standing emoji sequences do not qualify, but under a broader definition of socially learned combination constraints they show "syntax-like behavior" that remains "probabilistic, shallow, context-sensitive, and community-dependent." The paper's own framing is that emoji sequencing is "more than decoration" but explicitly not a grammar in the strict sense.

**Verdict on this system:** two-tier. Within a single emoji cluster, Unicode enforces a genuine, formal, machine-readable grammar. Across a message's sequence of standalone emoji, there is no formal grammar at all — only emergent, statistical, community convention. Treating "emoji has ZWJ grammar" as evidence that emoji messages are compositionally structured conflates these two very different levels.

## 6. Road and wayfinding pictogram systems

Public wayfinding pictograms are close to the flat-lookup end of the spectrum, with narrow, specific compositional exceptions.

**Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals (UNECE, 1968/2006):** organizes signs into annexes for danger warning, priority, prohibitory/restrictive, mandatory, special regulation, and informational/facility signs ([UNECE Consolidated Resolution](https://unece.org/DAM/trans/roadsafe/publications/docs/Consolidated_Resolution_on_Road_Traffic_RE2_e.pdf); [Vienna Convention text](https://unece.org/fileadmin/DAM/trans/conventn/Conv_road_signs_2006v_EN.pdf)). A handful of pictograms are explicitly documented as composed from other existing base symbols (e.g., a specific pictogram is defined as combining the existing service-station symbol with an added element), but this is the exception, not a general combination rule, and Variable Message Sign guidance still favors dedicated single pictograms (e.g., a specific "congestion" icon) over generic warning shapes ([Wikipedia: Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_Road_Signs_and_Signals)).

**ISO 7001 (Public Information Symbols, 4th edition, 2023):** exactly 177 symbols across seven categories — accessibility, public facilities, transport facilities, behavior of the public, commercial facilities, tourism/cultural/heritage, and sporting activities ([Wikipedia: ISO 7001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7001); [ISO 7001:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/77442.html)). The one genuine, documented compositional operator in this standard is the **prohibition modifier**: a red diagonal slash covering the whole symbol, or a red cross covering the specific prohibited element, can be added to a base pictogram to produce "no X" (e.g., baggage cart + slash = "no baggage carts"). This is a real, if minimal, modifier-plus-base compositional mechanism, governed by the symbol-design standard ISO 22727:2007. Beyond this negation operator, ISO 7001 remains one-symbol-one-meaning.

**AIGA Symbol Signs:** 50 symbols (34 from 1974, 16 added in 1979), developed with the U.S. Department of Transportation and released copyright-free, still the U.S. signage-industry standard ([AIGA Symbol Signs](https://www.aiga.org/resources/symbol-signs)). The clearest compositional device here is the **arrow**, which is treated as a separate element combined with a base pictogram or text for directional wayfinding — documentation explicitly states directional arrows are "only to be used in conjunction with other symbols," and direction-sensitive pictograms (e.g., stairs, escalators) may be mirrored only when paired with a directional arrow. Even so, the underlying pictogram vocabulary itself is flat: each base icon still maps to exactly one meaning, with the arrow as the sole systematic compositional operator layered on top.

**Verdict on this system:** predominantly flat symbol lookup, with two narrow but real and standardized compositional exceptions — the prohibition-slash/cross modifier (ISO 7001) and the directional arrow (AIGA and general wayfinding practice).

## 7. Military and tactical symbology — NATO APP-6 / MIL-STD-2525

Military symbology is the strongest and most rigorously formalized counter-example to the flat-dictionary generalization identified in this review.

**Explicit compositional schema.** MIL-STD-2525D defines every symbol as an assembly of **Frame + Fill + Icon + Modifiers + Amplifiers**, built within a defined "virtual bounding octagon" through a specified sequence: (1) select the frame shape by standard identity (unknown/friend/neutral/hostile/etc.) crossed with battle dimension (air/land/sea/space/cyberspace) and status (solid outline = present, dashed = planned); (2) select the main icon; (3–4) optionally add up to two modifiers in defined sectors; (5) add amplifiers ([MIL-STD-2525D, jcs.mil](https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/Doctrine/Other_Pubs/ms_2525d.pdf)). Color redundantly encodes identity (hostile = red, friend = cyan/blue, neutral = green, unknown = yellow). More than 40 named amplifier fields (labeled A through AS) each carry defined semantics and character limits — echelon, quantity, reinforced/reduced status, direction of movement, speed, altitude, IFF codes, reliability ratings (A–F) and credibility ratings (1–6), and combat-effectiveness codes.

**Formal, bidirectional machine grammar.** The standard specifies the **Symbol Identification Code (SIDC)**, a fully positional numeric code — two sets of ten digits (twenty total), extendable with an optional ten-digit Set C for national/geopolitical extensions, for up to thirty digits — with precisely defined digit positions for version, standard identity, symbol set, status, headquarters/task-force flag, amplifier/descriptor, entity, entity type/subtype, and sector-1/sector-2 modifiers. Compliant systems are required to both assemble a full graphical symbol *from* a SIDC and generate a SIDC *from* an assembled symbol — a bidirectional parse/generate requirement that is functionally equivalent to a compiler grammar, not a lookup table.

**Explicit self-description as a shift away from flat lookup.** The standard states directly that "MIL-STD-2525D restructures the standard to build symbols from components rather than predefining all possible symbols" — an explicit, documented historical move *from* enumeration *to* composition. Wikipedia's account of NATO Joint Military Symbology adds an important nuance: earlier APP-6 versions attempted an exhaustive enumerated list of icons rather than a true component dictionary, which caused real operational failures — especially in Military Operations Other Than War (MOOTW) — when unforeseen situations required symbols nobody had predefined, a problem worsened by centralized, slow-moving maintenance ([Wikipedia: NATO Joint Military Symbology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Joint_Military_Symbology)). Even this strongest counter-example, in other words, had real extensibility friction before its deliberate move toward component-based composition; governance today spans NATO STANAG 2019 (APP-6, versions A through E, 1986–present) and the U.S. MIL-STD-2525 series (A through D), which are converging.

**Verdict on this system:** unambiguous compositional grammar with a formal, machine-parseable, bidirectional encoding — the clearest and most rigorously documented counter-example to "flat dictionary" in this entire review, and one that long predates any small modern pictogram project.

## 8. Diagrammatic visual DSLs — UML, BPMN, Scratch

Modern diagrammatic domain-specific languages provide a useful comparison class because they are, by design, machine-parseable grammars rather than pictogram sets, showing what a fully engineered compositional visual system looks like.

**UML:** standardized by the OMG as a metamodel built on the Meta-Object Facility, with a formal semantic specification, a graphical notation, an XML Metadata Interchange (XMI) serialization format, and well-formedness constraints expressed in the Object Constraint Language (OCL); also standardized as ISO/IEC 19501/19505 ([OMG UML specification](https://www.omg.org/spec/UML/ISO/19501/PDF); [Wikipedia: UML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unified_Modeling_Language)).

**BPMN 2.0:** an OMG specification shipped with actual machine-readable XML Schema files (BPMN20.xsd, BPMNDI.xsd, DC.xsd, DI.xsd, Semantic.xsd), making every valid diagram programmatically parseable ([OMG BPMN 2.0.2 specification](https://www.omg.org/spec/BPMN/2.0.2/PDF); [Camunda BPMN primer](https://docs.camunda.io/docs/components/modeler/bpmn/bpmn-primer/)). Three core Flow Object categories — Events (circle), Activities (rounded rectangle, with subtypes such as user/manual/service/send/receive/script/business-rule tasks), and Gateways (diamond, with exclusive/XOR, parallel/AND, inclusive/OR, and event-based variants) — connect via Sequence Flow arrows with formally defined source/target and execution semantics, executable in real workflow engines (Camunda, Zeebe, Flowable, jBPM) via a token-based flow model.

**Scratch:** grammar is enforced geometrically through block shapes — Hat (trigger), Stack (command), Boolean, Reporter (function), C-shaped (control), and Cap blocks only connect in combinations that are syntactically valid by shape, functionally equivalent to an EBNF grammar expressed spatially rather than textually ([DeepScratch paper](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume11No7/Paper_77-DeepScratch_Scratch_Programming_Language.pdf)). Scratch ships with 119 standard blocks as its base vocabulary, and its extension system (since 2015) allows new vocabulary (custom blocks implemented in JavaScript) to be added while the underlying connective grammar stays fixed — a clean illustration of vocabulary and grammar evolving independently. The same source frames this generally: "any visual programming language is characterized by two main elements: a grammar (syntactical definition) and a vocabulary (lexical definition)," which is the organizing principle this report applies to every pictographic system above.

**Verdict on this system:** fully compositional and formally machine-readable by construction — these are engineered languages, not naturally evolved pictogram sets, and represent the ceiling of what explicit visual grammar can look like.

## 9. Other constructed iconic-language projects

Several small modern projects explicitly attempt what Blissymbolics and military symbology achieved for different purposes: documented compositional grammar for general pictographic communication.

**Icono** (Peter Kramer, *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2023): an icon-based candidate universal written language with an explicitly documented graphical grammar ([Icono, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421668/)). Icono marks grammatical roles graphically rather than through affixes: a dotted line above a word marks the subject, a continuous line marks the verb, a dashed line marks the object or complement; negation is expressed by reversing figure/ground color (black-on-white becomes white-on-black); plurality and superlatives are marked by duplicating a constituent icon; modifiers are bracketed and positioned relative to the word they modify, explicitly analogized to a mathematical function \( f(x) \); numerals use dice-like dot configurations; subordinate clauses can nest iteratively. Vocabulary is compositional by design — new words are built by combining icons that share a semantic feature (e.g., a hummingbird icon plus an emu icon jointly denoting "bird" through what the two examples have in common), and by "determinative" icons that function as semantic-category markers analogous to Egyptian hieroglyphic determinatives (e.g., a gavel icon combined with other icons to build "judge," "court of law," or "judgment"). Kramer explicitly reviews Blissymbolics, ISOTYPE, PECS, Minspeak, iConji, Nobel, aUI, and IKON as comparators and argues that none of them combines a sufficiently large, extensible, non-cluttered vocabulary with a fully documented, sophisticated grammar — a direct, named claim that most prior iconic languages fall short of what Icono attempts.

**IKON / KomunIKON** (Cesco Reale's KomunIKON project; Meloni, Hitmeangsong, Appelhaus, Walthert & Reale, 2022): an active research project explicitly building "a grammatical and graphical framework" intended to translate natural-language sentences into icon sequences ([IKON paper, ojs.unito.it](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/ricognizioni/article/download/7095/6096/23236)). IKON explicitly claims morphological and syntactic rules, grammar categories analogous to Bliss, and the possibility of two-dimensional syntax modeled on LoCoS, and it draws on Chinese, Egyptian hieroglyphs, Maya script, Bliss, AAC systems, and Esperanto as design influences. Its compositional strategies are unusually well taxonomized: compound icons built via hyperonymy (a specific instance standing for a broader category), contrast, metonymy, and synecdoche; "correlative" icons formed by juxtaposing independently meaningful elements (e.g., WHAT = "which" + "object," WHEN = "which" + "time"); and "contextual" icons using bound morphological markers such as speed lines or speech balloons that cannot stand alone and must attach to a root icon. IKON was evaluated using the University of Geneva's Iconometer methodology on 73 participants across 23 countries and 28 languages, with accuracy for individual icons ranging from roughly 9% (a poorly performing "must" design) to over 50% average certainty for stronger designs such as "tax" — evidence that the project measures comprehension empirically rather than asserting it. However, the primary sources reviewed do not provide a total vocabulary count, a complete formal grammar specification, or any machine-readable schema, API, or parser for IKON — its grammar is described narratively and by example, not formally specified the way Unicode's UTS #51 or MIL-STD-2525's SIDC are.

**Toki Pona / Sitelen Pona** (Sonja Lang): included here as a contrast case rather than a genuine counter-example. Toki Pona is a spoken constructed language with roughly 137–120 words; its Sitelen Pona script is a **logography** — one glyph per word, structurally like Chinese characters — not a compositional pictogram grammar in the sense used throughout this report ([Wikipedia: Toki Pona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toki_Pona); [Wikipedia: Sitelen Pona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitelen_Pona)). It is included in the (unofficial) Under-ConScript Unicode Registry at U+F1900–U+F19FF. The grammar that makes Toki Pona expressive lives in its spoken word combination rules, not in its pictographic notation.

**Other named-but-thinly-documented systems:** Pictoperanto (Gros, 2011), iConji (a "free pictographic communication system based on an open visual vocabulary with built-in translations"), Minspeak (Baker, 1982, an AAC system using icon-sequence "compaction" to store many messages behind few icons), Nobel (Randic, 2019), aUI (Weilgart, 1979), and IconText (Beardon, 1995) are each referenced in the Icono and IKON literature but were not found with primary-source detail sufficient for a full seven-dimension assessment in this review ([Icono, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421668/)).

## Final assessment: is "flat dictionary" a fair generalization, and is "we have compositional grammar" a distinguishing claim?

The evidence gathered here does **not** support an unqualified claim that "most pictogram systems are flat dictionaries with no combination rules." That generalization is overstated, and the record shows why.

**Systems that clearly refute the flat-dictionary generalization, with genuine, well-documented compositional grammar predating any modern small project by decades:**

- **Blissymbolics** (1949–present) has explicit word-order syntax, morphological indicators for tense, plurality, passive voice, and comparison, a classifier+specifier compounding system, and a governing body (BCI) that has grown its authorized vocabulary generatively from roughly 900 base characters to 6,556 symbols — all specified in detail three-quarters of a century before any contemporary pictogram project ([BCI](https://www.blissymbolics.org/); [Evertype rules](https://www.evertype.com/standards/by/bliss-rules-20090126.pdf)).
- **NATO/US military symbology (APP-6 / MIL-STD-2525)** is, if anything, an even stronger counter-example: it has a fully specified Frame+Fill+Icon+Modifier+Amplifier composition schema and a formal, bidirectional, machine-parseable Symbol Identification Code, and the standard explicitly documents its own historical shift "from predefining all possible symbols" to "building symbols from components" ([MIL-STD-2525D](https://www.jcs.mil/Portals/36/Documents/Doctrine/Other_Pubs/ms_2525d.pdf)).
- **SignWriting** achieves genuine two-dimensional spatial compositionality — handshape, movement, location, and dynamics are independently meaningful primitives assembled per sign — and has been doing so since 1974 ([SignWriting](https://www.signwriting.org/)).
- Within its narrow scope, **Unicode's formal emoji grammar (UTS #51)** is a real, rigorously specified, machine-parseable compositional system, even though it does not extend to free-standing emoji sequences.
- **UML, BPMN, and Scratch** demonstrate that fully engineered compositional visual grammars, with machine-readable schemas, are a mature and unremarkable category of technology outside the pictogram-for-communication space specifically.

**Systems genuinely closer to flat lookup, where the "flat dictionary" characterization holds up well:**

- **ISOTYPE** is the clearest case: Neurath's own stated position was that it is "syntactically…limited and underdeveloped" and could never become a full language — a self-assessment, not an outside critique.
- **Commercial AAC symbol sets** (PCS/Boardmaker, ARASAAC, SymbolStix) function largely as flat, one-icon-one-meaning vocabularies at the symbol level; grammar, where it exists (PODD, PECS), is a property of the display or board software layered on top, not of the icons themselves.
- **Road and wayfinding pictograms** (ISO 7001, Vienna Convention, AIGA) are predominantly one-symbol-one-meaning, with only two narrow, standardized exceptions (the prohibition slash/cross and the directional arrow).
- **Emoji sequences across separate emoji** (as opposed to within a single ZWJ cluster) have no formal grammar at all — only statistically emergent, community-dependent convention, per the closest academic study available on the question.

**What this means for a small modern project's marketing claim.** Given this evidence, a claim of the shape "we are unusual because we have compositional grammar at all" is not defensible — Blissymbolics and military symbology had rigorously documented compositional grammars decades before any contemporary open-source pictogram project existed, and both remain far more extensively specified and governed than most modern efforts, including the academically-documented modern contenders Icono and IKON/KomunIKON. A more precise and defensible differentiation claim for a small modern project would instead emphasize characteristics that genuinely are rare in this review's sample, such as: **(a)** having a machine-readable, versioned, CI-validated grammar specification (a formal `GRAMMAR.md` and JSON message schema with automated tests) at a deliberately small vocabulary scale — most systems with comparably rigorous machine-readability (military symbology, UML/BPMN, Unicode emoji) operate at institutional scale with formal standards bodies, while most small-scale or individually authored pictogram projects (ISOTYPE historically, most AAC symbol sets, Icono and IKON as currently documented) have narrative or example-based grammars without a public automated validation pipeline; **(b)** explicitly separating embodied from standalone communication needs as a first-class design principle, which is not a documented feature of any other system reviewed here; and **(c)** achieving compositional negation, quantity, and relational operators with a genuinely small (~83-icon) vocabulary, which is a meaningfully different engineering trade-off than Blissymbolics' 6,556-symbol authorized vocabulary or Icono's projected ~1,000-icon target, even though it is not a claim to having invented compositionality itself. The intellectually honest framing is "small, explicit, machine-validated grammar at unusually small vocabulary scale," not "grammar where others have none" — the latter claim is not supported by the systems examined in this review, several of which have had rigorously documented, formally governed compositional grammars for anywhere from several decades (Blissymbolics, APP-6) to over a century's worth of institutional design tradition in the case of statistical pictography's broader lineage.
