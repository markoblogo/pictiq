# Pictiq Manifesto of Inclusive Design

## Preamble

Most of the visual languages we use every day — restroom signs, emoji, airport pictograms — do more than convey meaning. They quietly encode judgments about who counts as the default and who counts as the exception. A restroom sign decides that gender is binary. A family icon decides which family is the "real" one. An emoji skin tone decides which color is the default and which one has to be specially selected.

Pictiq is built differently, and not out of politeness — out of an engineering principle: a symbol system that claims to be universal has no right to pre-decide who, among the people speaking it, is the exception.

This is not a political manifesto. It is a manifesto about what data actually belongs in a language meant for everyone.

## Principle 1 — A person, not a gender

Pictiq does not distinguish a man from a woman as separate symbols. There is one neutral person icon. This is not a simplification for its own sake — it removes a variable that most real messages ("I need help", "a person is here") never require, and that has historically turned a simple wayfinding task into a decade-long design debate over gender-neutral signage in public spaces ([Australian Design Review](https://www.australiandesignreview.com/architecture/more-than-just-a-bathroom-sign-how-design-can-become-more-inclusive/); [Sociological Images](https://thesocietypages.org/socimages/2014/07/01/gender-neutral-bathrooms-solving-the-sign-problem/)).

## Principle 2 — A loved one, not a status

Pictiq does not encode "husband", "wife", "partner", or "boyfriend". There is one icon: a loved one. Marriage is a legal construct that differs by country and culture; sexual orientation is not information a symbol is entitled to disclose. A bond of love is universal and needs no legal or gendered qualifier to be understood.

## Principle 3 — Family as a group, not a template

Pictiq does not draw "a mother, a father, and two children" as the one true image of family. Family is a group of people connected by the loved-one icon, in any composition. The default nuclear-family template in public signage is a well-documented point of exclusion for single-parent, same-sex, adoptive, and extended families; Pictiq does not reproduce that default.

## Principle 4 — Citizenship and origin as fact, not race as a label

Pictiq has no symbol for race or ethnicity. It has a fact: citizenship (legal belonging to a country) and, separately, place of birth. These are distinct things, and both are facts rather than visual attributes of appearance. Emoji history is a direct cautionary tale here: the original Unicode set was fairly criticized for racial homogeneity, and the skin-tone modifiers introduced afterward became their own source of debate over what counts as a "neutral" default ([Miltner, 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/193224/1/MiltnerNMS2019OnePartPolitics_2.pdf); [UA News Center](https://news.ua.edu/2020/07/emoji-reveal-whiteness-as-driver-of-technology/)). Pictiq's answer is not to solve that problem more cleverly, but not to create it in the first place.

## Principle 5 — A need, not a disability label

Pictiq has no single "person with a disability" icon. Collapsing many different conditions and needs into one static image is exactly the reduction that led to the grassroots redesign of the classic wheelchair symbol through the Accessible Icon Project ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/blogs-ouch-24149316); [Media Cause — "Accessibility and the Harm of Simple Symbols"](https://mediacause.com/accessibility-the-harm-of-simple-symbols/)). Instead of an identity label, Pictiq encodes the specific functional need a person has right now — what they actually require, not what the system assigns them to be.

## Principle 6 — Age as a number, not a verdict

Pictiq does not use "old" or "young" as categories. Age is a number. Categorical age labels carry an evaluative, often ageist undertone, and they are less useful than a precise figure: "7" or "68" communicates more than "a child" or "an elderly person" ever could.

## Principle 7 — Grammar over an endless vocabulary

The tempting way to "solve" inclusivity is to add a separate symbol for every identity, situation, and combination. Pictiq takes the opposite path: a small vocabulary of facts and needs, combined with a composition grammar, can express far more than a sprawling list of categories ever could — without needing a new icon every time the world produces a new identity to describe. Neutrality here is not a goal pursued for its own sake; it is a direct consequence of keeping the system small through grammar rather than through a growing catalog of labels.

## Principle 8 — An open protocol, not a decision handed down

Pictiq does not claim to have settled the question of inclusive design once and for all. The vocabulary is versioned, and every extension passes through an open decision tree rather than being unilaterally assigned by one author. Every principle listed above can and should be challenged and revised by the community — this manifesto records a principle, not a final, closed list of excluded concepts.

## Closing

Pictiq is not a language that politely avoids uncomfortable topics. It is a language that, as a matter of engineering, refuses to assume who the "typical person" is before it starts talking to them.
